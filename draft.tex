\section{Motivation}

Stochastic gradient descent finds solutions to neural network training, but the nature of these solutions remains poorly understood. Networks trained from different initializations converge to different points in parameter space. Do these points lie in isolated basins, separated by high-loss barriers? Or do they all belong to a single connected region of low loss?


\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{example_loss_landscapev2.png}
    \caption{Example loss landscape in parameter space: optimization trajectories from different random initializations converge to distinct local minima}
    \label{fig:placeholder}
\end{figure}

Multiple isolated basins would mean that initialization is critical and that independently trained models occupy genuinely different, separated regions of the loss landscape. A single basin would mean that distinct solutions are fundamentally related, with their apparent diversity arising from symmetries in the parameterization.

This question has practical implications. If solutions lie in the same basin, they can be connected by linear paths along which the loss remains low. This enables \textbf{model merging}: combining separately trained models by averaging their weights, which is useful for federated learning where data cannot be centralized. It also enables \textbf{efficient ensembling}: sampling diverse models along low-loss paths rather than training multiple networks from scratch. Conversely, if solutions lie in isolated basins, linear interpolation between them crosses high-loss barriers, and weight averaging produces dysfunctional networks. Understanding the basin structure is therefore essential for determining when these practical techniques can succeed, while also providing valuable insight into loss landscape geometry.

\section{Problem Statement}

\citep{entezari2021role} conjectured that most SGD solutions can be permuted so that there is no barrier on the linear interpolation between them---the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis}. Formally:

\begin{quote}
\textit{For most pairs of SGD solutions $\theta_A$ and $\theta_B$, there exists a permutation $P$ such that the loss barrier on the linear path between $\theta_A$ and $P(\theta_B)$ is approximately zero.}
\end{quote}

This conjecture suggests that the loss landscape may be fundamentally simpler than it appears, with apparently distinct basins being permutations of the same underlying solution. However, the conjecture remains unproven, and existing empirical evidence is inconclusive. Moreover, the statement is inherently ambiguous—"most" is undefined, and there is no threshold specified for what constitutes "approximately zero" barrier.

The central question this thesis addresses is:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Do independently trained neural networks lie in the same basin of the loss landscape if permutation symmetries are taken into account?}
\end{quote}

\section{Limitations of Existing Evidence}

Current empirical tests of the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesi} face several limitations. 
First, successful demonstrations of linear mode connectivity have been restricted 
to specific architecture–dataset combinations. When alignment methods are applied 
to more challenging architectures such as VGG16 on CIFAR-10, they fail to achieve 
full linear mode connectivity—even when the networks achieve similar, high test 
accuracy and agree on the vast majority of predictions \citep{ainsworth2023git}, indicating highly similar predictive behavior. This raises a fundamental question:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Do alignment methods fail because they are unable to find the correct permutation within a vast combinatorial search space?
    \item Or do multiple basins genuinely exist, meaning that no permutation achieving linear mode connectivity exists for those models?
\end{itemize}

The difficulty of resolving this question theoretically comes from the intrinsic 
complexity of modern neural networks. Their loss landscapes are high-dimensional, 
non-convex, and exhibit symmetries due to parameter permutations and 
scaling invariances. As a result, rigorous global statements about basin structure 
are notoriously challenging to establish.

To conclusively prove or disprove the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis}, 
one would need to either:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Develop a robust and principled permutation-alignment method that consistently achieves
    linear mode connectivity across architectures and datasets, demonstrating that apparent
    barriers arise solely from misalignment in parameter space rather than from genuinely distinct basins, or
    \item Prove that for at least one non-degenerate architecture–dataset configuration, 
    no permutation within the full symmetry group yields linear mode connectivity, 
    thereby establishing the existence of genuinely distinct basins.
\end{itemize}

\section{Research Questions}

This thesis investigates the validity of the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis} through the following research questions:

\begin{enumerate}  
    \item[\textbf{RQ1}] \textbf{(Hypothesis Validity)} Do any two independently trained neural networks lie in the same basin modulo permutation?

    \item[\textbf{RQ2}] \textbf{(Method Limitations)} When alignment methods fail to achieve linear mode connectivity, is this due to limitations of the methods or evidence against the hypothesis?
\end{enumerate}
\noindent We assume throughout that networks share the same architecture, training set, loss function, and hyperparameters---differing only in random initialization and batch ordering. The specific architectures, datasets, and experimental configurations are detailed in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}.


\section{Contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Controlled Permutation Benchmark:} A methodology to validate permutation-finding methods by constructing pairs of networks where ground truth permutation is known by construction. This distinguishes method failure from hypothesis failure.
    
    \item \textbf{Same-Function Test (Pure XOR):} An experiment testing whether networks that compute exactly the same function (100\% accuracy on XOR) are linearly connected modulo permutation---a necessary condition for the hypothesis.
    

    \item \textbf{Distance-Connectivity Analysis:} Systematic study of how the distance between solutions in parameter space affects alignment quality and barrier height.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Definitions and Theoretical Foundations}
\label{sec:definitions} 
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Loss Landscapes}

The loss landscape of a neural network is the function $\mathcal{L}: \Theta \to \mathbb{R}$ mapping parameters $\theta \in \Theta$ to loss. Understanding the geometry of this landscape---particularly the structure of minima and the paths connecting them---is fundamental to understanding why neural network optimization succeeds.

\subsection{Basin}

A basin in the loss landscape is informally a connected region of parameter space containing a local minimum, where gradient-based optimization initialized within this region will converge to that minimum. More precisely, for a minimum $\theta^*$, its basin is the set of parameter initializations from which gradient descent converges to $\theta^*$.

For the purposes of mode connectivity, we use a relaxed definition: two solutions $\theta_A$ and $\theta_B$ are in the same basin if they can be connected by a continuous path along which the loss remains low (close to the endpoint losses). This definition focuses on the connectivity structure rather than the convergence behavior.


\subsection{Loss Barrier}

The loss barrier \citep{frankle2020linear} quantifies the difficulty of linearly interpolating between two solutions. Given two parameter vectors $\theta_A$ and $\theta_B$ with comparable loss:

\begin{equation}
\text{barrier}(\theta_A, \theta_B) = \max_{\lambda \in [0,1]} \mathcal{L}\!\left((1-\lambda)\theta_A + \lambda \theta_B\right)
 - \frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{L}(\theta_A) + \mathcal{L}(\theta_B)\right)
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{L}$ is the loss function (e.g., cross-entropy). The barrier is non-negative by definition and is typically approximated by evaluating the loss at discrete interpolation points (e.g., $\lambda = 0.0, 0.1, \ldots, 1.0$).

\textbf{Interpretation:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Barrier $\approx 0$: Flat or convex interpolation; solutions are linearly connected.
    \item Barrier $> 0$: A loss ``hill'' exists between solutions.
    \item Barrier $\approx$ loss at random initialization: Solutions are effectively disconnected.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{imabarrier_example_v3ge.png}
    \caption{Loss along the interpolation between two modes showing different barrier regimes.}
    \label{fig:placeholder}
\end{figure}
\subsection{Mode Connectivity}

Two solutions are \textbf{mode connected} if there exists a continuous path $\gamma : [0,1] \to \Theta$ with $\gamma(0)=\theta_A$ and $\gamma(1)=\theta_B$ such that:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\gamma(t)) \approx \mathcal{L}(\theta_A) \approx \mathcal{L}(\theta_B) \quad \forall\, t \in [0,1]
\end{equation}

This definition allows curved paths (Bézier curves, polygonal chains), which is less restrictive than linear mode connectivity.


\subsection{Linear Mode Connectivity}

Two networks $\theta_A$ and $\theta_B$ are \textbf{linearly mode connected} (LMC) if the loss along the linear interpolation $\theta_\lambda = (1-\lambda)\theta_A + \lambda\theta_B$ remains low for all $\lambda \in [0,1]$. The literature does not prescribe a universal threshold for ``low.'' In practice, LMC is typically considered to hold when the interpolated model remains functional---e.g., when test accuracy at any point along the interpolation stays within a few percentage points of the endpoint accuracies \citep{ainsworth2023git}, or equivalently, when the loss barrier is negligible compared to the loss at random initialization.

\subsection{Permutation Symmetry}

Neural networks admit many parameterizations that represent exactly the same function. The symmetry most relevant for this thesis is permutation symmetry: hidden units or channels may be reordered, as long as the next layer is adjusted accordingly \citep{hechtnielsen1990algebraic}. The same idea also appears in other architectures, for example in attention-based models where heads can often be permuted without changing the represented function. For the purposes of this thesis, however, it is sufficient to consider fully connected hidden layers and convolutional layers.

\paragraph{Hidden layers.}
For two consecutive fully connected layers,
\[
f(x) = W_2 \,\sigma(W_1 x),
\]
let $P$ be a permutation matrix acting on the hidden units. If the first layer is permuted on its output side and the second layer is compensated on its input side, then the function is unchanged:
\[
\widetilde{f}(x)
=
(W_2 P^\top)\,\sigma(P W_1 x)
=
W_2 P^\top P \,\sigma(W_1 x)
=
W_2 \sigma(W_1 x)
=
f(x).
\]
Thus, reordering hidden neurons and undoing the same reordering in the next layer leaves the network invariant.

\paragraph{Convolutional layers.}
The same argument holds for convolutional channels. Let the first convolution produce hidden feature maps
\[
h = \sigma(W_1 * x),
\]
and let $P$ permute the output channels of this layer. Then the permuted hidden representation is
\[
\widetilde{h} = \sigma((P W_1) * x) = P h,
\]
and the next convolution can compensate by permuting its input-channel dimension with $P^\top$:
\[
(W_2 P^\top) * \widetilde{h}
=
(W_2 P^\top) * (P h)
=
W_2 * h.
\]
Therefore, permuting the channels of one convolutional layer and applying the inverse permutation to the input channels of the next layer does not change the network function.

In both cases, permutations can be chosen independently at each hidden layer or hidden channel dimension. This creates a large family of parameter vectors that are functionally identical, even though they may be far apart in Euclidean parameter space.

\subsection{The One-Basin Modulo Permutation Hypothesis}

\citep{entezari2021role} conjectured that most SGD solutions belong to a set whose elements can be permuted so that linear interpolation between any two permuted elements has low barrier---the one-basin modulo permtuation hypothesis. Formally, for most pairs of SGD solutions $\theta_A, \theta_B$:
\begin{equation}
\exists\, P \in \mathcal{P} \text{ such that } \theta_A \text{ and } P(\theta_B) \text{ are linearly mode connected.}
\end{equation}
This accounts for the fact that $\theta_B$ and $P(\theta_B)$ compute the same function but occupy different locations in parameter space due to permutation symmetry. Importantly, even if $\theta_A$ and $P(\theta_B)$ are LMC, they may still compute different functions---the hypothesis claims only that they lie in the same low-loss basin, not that all SGD solutions are functionally equivalent.

\subsection{Hierarchy of Connectivity Claims}

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Hierarchy of connectivity claims, from weakest to strongest.}
    \label{tab:hierarchy}
    \begin{tabular}{l l l}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Claim} & \textbf{Statement} & \textbf{Status} \\
        \midrule

    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis} corresponds to ``LMC modulo permutation''---stronger than general mode connectivity but weaker than claiming raw linear connectivity.

\section{Evidence For and Against the One-Basin Modulo Permutation Hypothesis}
\label{sec:literature}
%==============================================================================

This section traces the development of mode connectivity research, from early results establishing curved-path connectivity through modern permutation-finding algorithms. Each result is contextualized by its contribution to understanding the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis}.

\subsection{Mode Connectivity via Curved Paths}

The study of mode connectivity began with the discovery that trained networks can be connected by low-loss \textit{curved} paths, even when linear interpolation fails.

\citet{draxler2018essentially} used the Nudged Elastic Band (NEB) algorithm from computational chemistry to find minimum-energy paths between networks trained on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100. Their key finding: ``minima are perhaps best seen as points on a single connected manifold of low loss, rather than as the bottoms of distinct valleys.''

\citet{garipov2018loss} showed that simple parametric curves suffice to connect minima. They proposed two approaches:

\textit{Quadratic Bézier curve:}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bezier}
\theta(t) = (1-t)^2 \theta_A + 2t(1-t)\theta_{\text{mid}} + t^2 \theta_B
\end{equation}

\textit{Polygonal chain:}
\begin{equation}
\theta(t) = \begin{cases}
2(t\theta_{\text{mid}} + (0.5 - t)\theta_A) & 0 \leq t \leq 0.5 \\
2((t - 0.5)\theta_B + (1 - t)\theta_{\text{mid}}) & 0.5 \leq t \leq 1
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $\theta_{\text{mid}}$ is a trainable midpoint optimized to minimize the expected loss along the path.

These results establish that minima are connected, suggesting the loss landscape has a simpler structure than previously thought. However, the existence of curved paths does not imply LMC---even a single bend point means the path deviates from the direct linear interpolation, leaving open whether linear connectivity could be achieved without such detours.



\subsection{The Entezari Conjecture and Statistical Evidence}

\citep{entezari2021role} proposed the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis} and provided indirect statistical evidence. Their key observation: the distribution of barriers between independently trained networks is statistically indistinguishable from the distribution of barriers between random permutations of the \textit{same} network.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{enzari.png}
    \caption{\textbf{Left:} Permuting hidden units of networks B, C, D to B', C', D' may eliminate barriers with A while preserving function. \textbf{Middle:} The hypothesis models independently trained networks as random permutations of the same underlying solution. \textbf{Right:} Barrier distributions between independent networks match those between random permutations of the same network across 3000+ networks. Points in the lower left mostly correspond to lowest barrier found after searching in the space of valid permutations using a Simulated Annealing algorithm.}
    \label{fig:entezari}
\end{figure}

The interpretation: if independently trained networks ``look like'' permuted versions of each other in terms of barrier statistics, they may actually \textit{be} permuted versions of the same underlying solution. This provides the first empirical motivation for the conjecture. However, the evidence is \textit{indirect}---the authors did not find permutations that actually achieve low barriers; they only showed that barrier distributions match. The evidence is insufficient, rather unmatching distributions of barrier would be a more convincing evidence of the incorrectness of the hypothesis. The hypothesis remained unverified: could such permutations actually be found?

\section{Architectures and Datasets}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Standard benchmarks:} VGG16 ($\sim$15M parameters) on CIFAR-10. These replicate prior work and validate our implementation. Three independently trained models (seed0, seed1, seed2) serve as endpoints.
    
    \item \textbf{Controlled synthetic:} Small MLPs (e.g., 2-H-1) with varying H on XOR. These enable direct hypothesis testing.
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

All interpolation paths are evaluated at 61 uniformly spaced points $\lambda \in [0, 1]$. At each point:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Compute interpolated weights: $\theta_\lambda = (1-\lambda)\theta_A + \lambda\theta_B$
    \item Recalculate batch normalization statistics on training set
    \item Record training loss, test loss, training accuracy, test accuracy
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Path-Finding Methods (Baseline)}

To establish that mode connectivity (curved paths) holds for our models, we use Bézier curve and Polygon chain with one trainable midpoint. These methods confirm that curved low-loss paths exist, consistent with prior work. The thesis then focuses on whether linear connectivity holds after permutation alignment.

\subsection{Permutation Alignment Methods}

We evaluate the following methods:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Weight Matching} \citep{ainsworth2023git}: Match neurons by weight similarity, solved as a Linear Assignment Problem (LAP) using the Hungarian algorithm.    \item \textbf{Activation Matching} \citep{ainsworth2023git}: Match neurons by activation correlation.
    \item \textbf{REPAIR} \citep{jordan2023repair}: Post-hoc activation rescaling.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Mode Connectivity via Curved Paths}

First, we compare low-loss nonlinear connectivity in two settings that are central to the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation} hypothesis. We consider pairs of independently trained models initialized from different random seeds. Second, we consider a model together with a permuted version of itself. This mirrors the comparison made by \citep{entezari2021role} in Figure~\ref{fig:entezari}, who studied barrier distributions for independently trained networks and for networks matched by permutation. Here, instead of focusing only on linear interpolation, we ask whether the same two scenarios also exhibit similarly low barriers along learned nonlinear paths. If they do not, that would suggest that the two cases may not be geometrically equivalent, despite the permutation-based hypothesis.

To test this, we train Bézier curves in two settings: (1) between independently initialized models (`seed0--seed1`), and (2) between a model and a permuted version of itself (`seed0--mirror`). Since low-loss curved paths are already known to exist between independently trained models \citep{garipov2018loss}, one would expect such paths to exist at least as readily between a model and a permuted copy, because the two endpoints implement the same function and differ only by a hidden-unit permutation. 

In the mirror construction, the permuted model is obtained by reversing the order of neurons within each hidden layer:
\[
\bigl(P_\ell^{\mathrm{mirror}}\bigr)_{ij} =
\begin{cases}
1, & j = n_\ell + 1 - i, \\
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\]
Here, $n_\ell$ is the number of hidden units in layer $\ell$, while $i$ and $j$ index the rows and columns of the permutation matrix. The rule $j = n_\ell + 1 - i$ simply encodes reversal of the hidden-unit ordering. The same construction applies to convolutional layers, where it reverses the order of output channels.

\section{Hyperplane-Constrained Path Finding}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{canvaxd_v2.png}
    \caption{Schematic of path optimization under a hyperplane constraint. The midpoint between two solutions is initialized on the linear interpolation and restricted to move inside a fixed hyperplane. A path is then obtained by connecting the endpoints through the optimized midpoint.}
    \label{fig:hyperplane_constraint_schematic}
\end{figure}



The Bézier experiments above show that low-loss curved paths can be found both between independently trained models and between models related by permutation. This raises a more geometric question: how much freedom is actually needed to find such paths? If low-loss paths are rare and occupy only narrow corridors in parameter space, then even a simple geometric constraint should make path optimization much harder. Conversely, if low-loss paths are abundant, then imposing a weak constraint should have little effect.

To test this, we constrained the midpoint of a three-bend polygonal chain. For two endpoints $w_0$ and $w_1$, the unconstrained polygonal path has one trainable midpoint $\theta$, and the path is obtained by connecting $w_0$ to $\theta$ and then $\theta$ to $w_1$. We modify this procedure by requiring $\theta$ to lie in a fixed hyperplane. After each gradient step, the midpoint is projected back onto the hyperplane, so optimization can still search for a low-loss path, but only within the allowed subspace.

As a first choice, we use the perpendicular bisector of the two endpoints. Let
\[
\mathbf{m} = \frac{1}{2}(w_0+w_1),
\qquad
\mathbf{n} = w_1-w_0.
\]
The constrained midpoint is required to satisfy
\[
(\theta-\mathbf{m})\cdot \mathbf{n}=0.
\]
This hyperplane is a natural geometric baseline because it passes through the midpoint of the interpolation and contains all points equidistant from the two endpoints. Importantly, we do not assume that it is a true symmetry plane of the loss landscape; it is simply a simple, structured codimension-1 constraint.

The constraint was verified using the normalized distance
\[
d_{\mathrm{norm}} =
\frac{|(\theta - \mathbf{m}) \cdot \mathbf{n}|}
{\|\mathbf{n}\| \cdot \sqrt{N}},
\]
which remained at machine precision ($d_{\mathrm{norm}} \approx 7.2 \times 10^{-8}$).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{random_planes-comp.png}
    \caption{Comparison of polygonal path optimization with different midpoint constraints. The midpoint is either unconstrained, constrained to the perpendicular bisector, or constrained to random hyperplanes.}
    \label{fig:random_planes_comparison}
\end{figure}



The result is that constraining the midpoint to a hyperplane does not substantially change the ability to find low-loss paths when the full path is optimized. Moreover, the perpendicular bisector behaves similarly to random hyperplanes, including random planes through the midpoint and random planes with random anchors. This suggests that the perpendicular bisector is not special for path finding in this setting.

The interpretation is that a codimension-1 constraint is very weak in a parameter space with roughly $15$ million dimensions. Removing one direction still leaves an enormous search space, so the optimizer can find low-loss paths almost as easily inside an arbitrary $(N-1)$-dimensional subspace. Thus, the main conclusion is not that a particular hyperplane has special geometric meaning, but that low-loss curved paths are highly abundant.

This supports a broader picture in which trained solutions are not connected only by delicate, low-dimensional corridors. Instead, the low-loss region appears thick enough that even constrained path searches retain enough freedom to succeed. A natural next question is how far this can be pushed: at what codimension does connectivity begin to degrade? Systematically reducing the dimension of the allowed subspace would provide a more direct way to estimate the effective thickness of the low-loss manifold.

    


\section{Controlled LMC Benchmark with Known Permutations}
In contrast to the Bézier experiments above, which test the existence of curved low-loss paths (mode connectivity), the following experiment focuses on linear mode connectivity (LMC) via straight-line interpolation. LMC is a strictly stronger geometric condition than curved connectivity: failure of LMC does not imply disconnection, but rather linear separation.

The goal of this experiment is to evaluate whether permutation-finding methods succeed when ground truth is available. Prior work has not evaluated alignment methods against known ground-truth permutations. Existing evaluations typically measure only the final barrier after alignment; if the barrier remains high, it is unclear whether (a) the method failed to recover a good permutation that exists, or (b) no such permutation exists, meaning the networks genuinely lie in different basins.

Importantly, prior work \citep{entezari2021role} focuses on aligning a model with a permuted version of itself—i.e., recovering the exact symmetry that makes two networks functionally identical. In that setting, successful alignment reduces one network to the other up to permutation. Our objective is different: we seek a permutation that aligns two \emph{distinct trained modes} so that they become linearly mode connected, not necessarily identical. The task is therefore not to reconstruct an exact symmetry transformation, but to find a permutation that reduces the LMC barrier between genuinely different solutions.

To create a controlled benchmark, we apply a known permutation $P^*$ to one network in a pair that is already LMC-connected, and test whether the alignment method can recover connectivity. Because a correct permutation is guaranteed to exist, success validates the method’s ability to recover meaningful alignments. Conversely, failure on this controlled task would indicate that the method is too weak to draw conclusions about the hypothesis from its performance. We adopted the shared initialization with split training approach from \citep{frankle2020linear} to construct these network pairs and weight matching \citep{ainsworth2023git} as a permutation finding method:


We fix $N = 200$ total epochs (the number required for this model to converge) and vary $x$ (the split point) to control the distance between $w_0$ and $w_1$. Longer shared training (larger $x$) produces closer networks while shorter shared training makes them more distant.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Train a model for $x$ epochs with a fixed batch ordering, producing a shared checkpoint $w_{\mathrm{shared}}$.
    \item Create two copies of $w_{\mathrm{shared}}$ and continue training them independently for $N-x$ epochs using different batch orderings. This produces two related endpoints, $w_0$ and $w_1$. \bluecircle{}
    \item Apply a known random permutation $P^\ast$ to one endpoint, giving $w_1' = P^\ast(w_1)$.
    \item Run weight matching between $w_0$ and the permuted endpoint $w_1'$ to recover an estimated alignment $P$.
    \item Test whether the recovered alignment removes the artificial permutation by comparing the original $\operatorname{barrier}(w_0, w_1)$ with the aligned $\operatorname{barrier}\bigl(w_0, P(w_1')\bigr)$. \greencircle{}
\end{enumerate}

In every case the recovered $\text{barrier}(w_0, P(w_1'))$ is lower than the original $\text{barrier}(w_0, w_1)$, suggesting the alignment method finds permutations that improves connectivity beyond the ground truth. This confirms that the method works correctly and manages to find an optimal permutation, at least when one exists.

The important observation is that weight matching is always applied to the pair $(w_0,w_1')$, where $w_1' = P^\ast(w_1)$ is obtained by applying a random permutation to $w_1$. Because of this artificial permutation, the apparent parameter-space mismatch between $w_0$ and $w_1'$ is large in all split settings, and is comparable to the mismatch between independently initialized models. Nevertheless, the quality of the recovered alignment depends strongly on how the underlying pair $(w_0,w_1)$ was created. When the two models share most of their training trajectory, alignment recovers a low-barrier connection. When the split occurs earlier and the models train independently for longer, the post-alignment barrier remains much larger. Thus, even though weight matching sees a similarly difficult-looking permuted pair in all cases, it succeeds only when the underlying unpermuted endpoints are already close.

This is precisely why the controlled benchmark is useful. For each shared-training split, we know that the artificial permutation can be undone: applying $(P^\ast)^{-1}$ to $w_1'$ recovers the original endpoint $w_1$. Therefore, each run comes with a guaranteed reference barrier, namely the barrier of the constructed pair $(w_0,w_1)$ before the random permutation was applied. The five runs differ only in how far apart the underlying endpoints are, controlled by the amount of shared training. After the artificial permutation, however, all runs present weight matching with the same kind of task: align $w_0$ to a randomly permuted version of another trained model. If permutation symmetry were sufficient to recover LMC between arbitrary modes, one might expect these controlled cases to yield comparable results after alignment, since each contains, in theory, a permutation that cancels out the permutation responsible for breaking LMC. Instead, weight matching succeeds only in cases where we know such an optimal permutation exists by construction, but not in cases where this is unknown; this raises the question of whether such permutations exist in the latter setting at all.

Moreover, we found that at distance $\approx 35$ corresponding to the $8/192$ split, the models are only weakly LMC-connected, with barriers of around 20\% accuracy error. Thus, even for shared-initialization pairs linear mode connectivity degrades substantially at large parameter distances. This reflects an intrinsic limitation of LMC at large separations in parameter space. In other words, LMC after permutation does not hold because the recovered permutation leaves the aligned models too far apart. If an optimal permutation truly places two modes within the same linear basin, one would expect their post-alignment distance to be small, at least for a weight-matching method. The persistence of large distances after alignment therefore still suggests either that no such permutation exists, or that current alignment methods fail to recover it. This raises a natural question: if LMC already becomes fragile at this scale, can we reasonably expect permutation alignment to achieve LMC for independently initialized networks whose post-alignment distance is comparable, if we cannot even construct modes with this distance mismatch and no barrier between them?

The analysis above leaves a few possible interpretations. One is that the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis} is too strong: permutation symmetry alone may not be sufficient to make independently trained modes linearly connected, since the alignment method fails to find suitable permutations for them but succeeds when such a permutation is guaranteed to exist. Another possibility is that the constructed shared-training models with later splits are easier for weight matching to align in some implicit, non-obvious way.



\section{XOR Experiment: Validating Alignment on a Minimal Problem}

The experiment above shows that alignment performance depends strongly on the underlying distance between modes. However, it still does not fully resolve whether failures at larger distances are caused by limitations of the alignment algorithm or by the absence of any permutation that would yield LMC. To separate these possibilities, we next study the XOR problem, which provides a minimal and fully analyzable setting for permutation alignment. With a 2-2-1 architecture, there are only two hidden-unit permutations: the identity and a swap. Exhaustive search is therefore trivial, allowing us to verify exactly when alignment succeeds or fails, and why.


Additionally, XOR is interesting because multiple distinct decision boundaries can achieve 100\% classification accuracy. Networks may learn different weight configurations while computing functions that agree on the four training points but differ elsewhere. This allows us to test alignment between networks with both similar and different decision boundaries.


We use the 4-point XOR problem centered at the origin ($\{(\pm 1, \pm 1)\}$) with a 2-2-1 MLP using ReLU activations and biases trained with SGD. Multiple networks are trained from independent random initializations until they achieve 100\% accuracy and near-zero loss. Up to 10 networks were trained which resulted in 45 pairs.

\section{XOR Experiment: Validating Mode Connectivity on a Minimal Problem}

The permutation-alignment experiment above showed that LMC fails after permutation allignment for most pairs in the minimal 2–2–1 XOR architecture. However, failure of linear mode connectivity does not imply the absence of curved connectivity. To determine whether the observed failures reflect genuine structural disconnection or simply the absence of LMC after permutation alignment, we next directly investigate curved mode connectivity in the same minimal setting. We attempted to find low-loss curved paths between independently trained XOR solutions using both Bézier curves and polygonal chains. Initially, we used a single trainable midpoint. When this failed to produce low-loss connections, we progressively increased the number of trainable points (up to 10), greatly increasing the flexibility of the path parameterization.

Despite this, low-loss paths were found only between models that were virtually identical in their weights (i.e., trivial connections). For non-trivial pairs, no method succeeded in discovering a low-loss curved path. This held even for models whose decision boundaries appeared visually identical. Thus, in the minimal-width 2-2-1 architecture, two independently trained solutions cannot in general be connected, even nonlinearly, unless they are already nearly identical in parameter space.

These results mirror our earlier permutation-alignment experiment on the minimal XOR architecture: alignment succeeded only in degenerate cases where the networks were effectively identical up to permutation. The absence of curved connectivity here suggests that width $m=2$ provides insufficient redundancy to allow different initializations to lie within the same connected low-loss manifold.

\subsubsection{Effect of Increasing Width}

To determine at what width the solution set becomes connected, we increased the hidden layer to $m=3$ neurons (2--3--1 architecture). With only one additional hidden unit, all independently trained models were successfully connected by low-loss curved paths, regardless of differences in their internal weight configurations or decision boundaries.

Indeed, a piecewise construction (a polygonal chain passing through the intermediate solution with 3 trainable points) would connect them by concatenating the two low-loss segments.

The failure of direct optimization in this case indicates that low-loss paths in the $m=3$ setting, while existent, may get stuck in local minima and therefore be difficult to find with a limited path parameterization. This highlights that low-loss path-finding algorithms are imperfect: failure to discover a low-loss path does not necessarily imply its non-existence, particularly in narrow networks with limited representational redundancy.



Overall, these results demonstrate a sharp transition in connectivity behavior between width $m=2$ and $m=3$. Minimal-width XOR solutions appear genuinely disconnected in parameter space (except for trivial cases), whereas adding a single hidden neuron introduces sufficient redundancy to render the solution set connected under curved paths.

\chapter{Beyond Permutation: Scale-Aware Alignment for Linear Mode Connectivity}
\label{chap:symmetry-aware}
%==============================================================================

The previous chapters showed that the central difficulty is not the existence of low-loss connections in general, but the much stronger requirement of linear mode connectivity. Curved low-loss paths were easy to find, even under substantial geometric constraints, which suggests that the loss landscape contains a broad connected low-loss manifold. However, the linear path between two solutions is much more restrictive. In the controlled permutation benchmark, we showed that permutation alignment works when a good permutation is guaranteed to exist, but that its quality degrades as the distance between solutions grows. In the XOR setting, we further observed that even the best possible permutation does not always eliminate the barrier completely.

These results suggest that failure of permutation-based alignment may not be explained solely by shortcomings of the optimization algorithm. Another possibility is that permutation invariance does not capture the full symmetry structure relevant for low-barrier interpolation. Neural networks with positively homogeneous activations such as ReLU possess an additional layerwise scaling symmetry: hidden units can be rescaled by positive constants provided the adjacent layer compensates appropriately, leaving the represented function unchanged. This motivates the next question of the thesis:

\begin{quote}
Can linear mode connectivity be improved by aligning networks modulo a larger symmetry class that includes both permutation and positive scaling?
\end{quote}

The goal is not merely to make two networks appear closer in parameter space. Rather, the objective is to find a symmetry-transformed representative of one network such that the linear interpolation to the other network has lower loss barrier. This chapter therefore extends the permutation-only perspective of the previous chapters into a broader symmetry-aware framework.

\section{Why Go Beyond Permutation?}

Permutation symmetry explains why two networks that compute similar features may nevertheless appear far apart in parameter space: corresponding hidden units may simply be ordered differently. However, even after a good correspondence has been found, the internal magnitude assigned to each hidden unit may still differ substantially across the two networks. For ReLU networks this magnitude is not uniquely identifiable, because one may multiply the outgoing activation of a hidden unit by a positive constant and divide the corresponding incoming weights of the next layer by the same constant without changing the function.

This observation is especially relevant in light of the XOR experiments from the previous chapter. There, exhaustive search over all permutations made it possible to identify the best achievable permutation exactly. Yet even after this oracle permutation was applied, a nonzero interpolation barrier often remained. This suggests that permutation symmetry alone may be insufficient: some of the residual barrier may come from mismatched internal scalings rather than mismatched neuron identities.

The present chapter investigates this possibility using a scale-augmented version of Sinkhorn re-basin. The chapter has two goals:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item to test whether adding positive scaling reduces the interpolation barrier relative to permutation-only alignment, and
    \item to determine whether any observed gain from scaling reflects a genuinely richer symmetry class rather than merely an easier optimization procedure.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Why Sinkhorn is the Best Base Method for Scale Augmentation}

Among the main re-basin families, Sinkhorn is the most natural method to extend with scaling. The reason is not that other methods are impossible to modify, but that their optimization structure makes a minimal scale-aware extension much less clean.

\paragraph{Compared with activation matching.}
Activation matching is formulated as a linear assignment problem (LAP). For activations \(Z^{(A)}, Z^{(B)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}\) at layer \(\ell\), the permutation is chosen by
\[
P_\ell
=
\arg\min_{P \in S_d}
\sum_{i=1}^n \|Z^{(A)}_{:,i} - P Z^{(B)}_{:,i}\|^2
=
\arg\max_{P \in S_d}
\langle P, Z^{(A)}(Z^{(B)})^\top \rangle_F.
\]
If scale variables are added, the activation statistics themselves change under scaling, so the similarity matrix is no longer fixed independently of the scale parameters. This makes the extension conceptually less direct.

\paragraph{Compared with weight matching.}
Weight matching is formulated as a coupled optimization over layerwise permutations:
\[
\arg\min_{\pi} \|\mathrm{vec}(\Theta^A) - \mathrm{vec}(\pi(\Theta^B))\|^2
=
\arg\max_{\pi} \mathrm{vec}(\Theta^A)\cdot \mathrm{vec}(\pi(\Theta^B)).
\]
Written in terms of the weights, this becomes
\[
\arg\max_{\pi=\{P_i\}}
\langle W_1^{(A)}, P_1 W_1^{(B)} \rangle_F
+
\langle W_2^{(A)}, P_2 W_2^{(B)} P_1^\top \rangle_F
+ \cdots +
\langle W_L^{(A)}, W_L^{(B)} P_{L-1}^\top \rangle_F.
\]
This is the sum of bilinear assignments problem (SOBLAP). If one augments each permutation by a diagonal scaling matrix, the transformed weights become coupled through both scaling factors and their inverses in adjacent layers. The resulting objective is no longer a clean sequence of assignment subproblems, so the scale-aware extension becomes structurally much more invasive.

\paragraph{Compared with STE-based methods.}
The straight-through estimator (STE) variant of Git Re-Basin directly optimizes an interpolation-related loss:
\[
\min_{\widetilde{\Theta}_B}
\mathcal{L}\!\left(
\frac{1}{2}
\left(
\Theta^A + \mathrm{proj}(\widetilde{\Theta}_B)
\right)
\right),
\qquad
\mathrm{proj}(\Theta)
=
\arg\max_{\pi}
\mathrm{vec}(\Theta)\cdot \mathrm{vec}(\pi(\Theta^B)).
\]
To incorporate scale, one would replace the permutation action by a mixed discrete-continuous transformation
\[
M_\ell = P_\ell D_\ell,
\qquad
D_\ell = \mathrm{diag}(e^{u_\ell}),
\]
and define transformed weights through
\[
W_\ell' = M_\ell W_\ell^B M_{\ell-1}^{-1}.
\]
This is possible in principle, but it mixes straight-through discrete optimization with continuous scale variables and inverse transformations across layers. As a first scale-aware extension, this is less clean than Sinkhorn.

\section{Why Sinkhorn is a Natural Starting Point}

Sinkhorn re-basin already replaces hard layerwise permutations with differentiable soft permutations. For each hidden layer \(\ell\), it learns a matrix of logits \(Z_\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{n_\ell \times n_\ell}\), where \(n_\ell\) is the width of the layer. The entries of \(Z_\ell\) are unconstrained scores indicating which units are likely to correspond across the two networks.

From \(Z_\ell\), the method computes a doubly stochastic matrix
\[
S_\ell = \mathrm{Sinkhorn}(Z_\ell / \tau),
\]
where \(\tau > 0\) is the temperature parameter. The Sinkhorn operator converts the score matrix into a soft permutation matrix: a nonnegative matrix whose rows and columns each sum to one. When \(\tau\) is small, the matrix becomes sharper and approaches a hard permutation; when \(\tau\) is larger, the alignment remains diffuse. This relaxes the original discrete search over permutations into a continuous optimization over the Birkhoff polytope.

The original Sinkhorn procedure therefore consists of:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item initializing layerwise logits \(Z_\ell\),
    \item computing soft alignments \(S_\ell = \mathrm{Sinkhorn}(Z_\ell / \tau)\),
    \item inserting these alignments consistently across adjacent layers,
    \item interpolating between model \(A\) and the transformed version of model \(B\),
    \item evaluating an interpolation-related objective, such as midpoint loss, and
    \item updating \(Z_\ell\) by gradient descent.
\end{enumerate}

Its main advantage in the present context is that the alignment variables are already optimized in a differentiable way under the actual quantity of interest, namely interpolation loss. This makes Sinkhorn the most natural base method for adding a second continuous symmetry: scaling.

\section{Scale-Aware Extension of Sinkhorn Re-Basin}

The proposed extension is intentionally minimal. Instead of using only a soft permutation matrix \(S_\ell\), each hidden layer is augmented with a positive diagonal scaling matrix
\[
D_\ell = \mathrm{diag}(e^{u_\ell}),
\]
where \(u_\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{n_\ell}\) are learnable log-scale parameters and the exponential ensures positivity. The layerwise alignment transformation becomes
\[
M_\ell = S_\ell D_\ell.
\]

The important point is that this leaves the overall Sinkhorn framework essentially unchanged. Both components,
the permutation structure \(Z_\ell \mapsto S_\ell\) and the scale structure \(u_\ell \mapsto D_\ell\), are continuous and can therefore be optimized jointly in the same gradient-based loop and under the same interpolation-loss objective. In this sense, the extension is a clean ablation of the original method:
\[
S_\ell
\quad \longrightarrow \quad
M_\ell = S_\ell D_\ell.
\]

This directly tests the following question:
\begin{quote}
Does adding scaling symmetry to Sinkhorn re-basin reduce interpolation barrier relative to permutation-only Sinkhorn?
\end{quote}

\section{Mathematical Formulation}

Let \(A\) and \(B\) be two networks with the same architecture. For each hidden layer \(\ell\), define
\[
S_\ell = \mathrm{Sinkhorn}(Z_\ell / \tau),
\qquad
D_\ell = \mathrm{diag}(e^{u_\ell}),
\qquad
M_\ell = S_\ell D_\ell.
\]

The transformed version of model \(B\) is constructed by applying the hidden-coordinate change consistently across adjacent layers:
\[
W_1' = M_1 W_1^B,
\]
\[
W_\ell' = M_\ell W_\ell^B M_{\ell-1}^{-1},
\qquad \ell = 2, \dots, L-1,
\]
\[
W_L' = W_L^B M_{L-1}^{-1}.
\]
For hidden biases,
\[
b_\ell' = M_\ell b_\ell^B.
\]

Given the transformed model \(B'\), define an interpolation grid
\[
\mathcal{A} = \{0.25, 0.5, 0.75\},
\]
and interpolated parameters
\[
\theta_\alpha = (1-\alpha)\theta^A + \alpha \theta^{B'}.
\]

The barrier objective is approximated by the average interpolation loss
\[
J_{\mathrm{bar}} =
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}
\sum_{\alpha \in \mathcal{A}}
\mathcal{L}(\theta_\alpha).
\]

To keep the learned scales close to the identity unless the data support otherwise, we add a small penalty on the log-scales:
\[
J_{\mathrm{scale}} =
\sum_{\ell} \|u_\ell\|_2^2.
\]

The total objective is therefore
\[
J = J_{\mathrm{bar}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{scale}} J_{\mathrm{scale}}.
\]

\section{Experimental Modes}

To distinguish the effect of scaling from the effect of optimization strategy, four experimental modes were considered.

\paragraph{permutation\_only}
Train only the Sinkhorn permutation variables. No scale variables are active. This is the primary baseline and shows how much can be achieved using permutation symmetry alone.

\paragraph{joint\_scale}
Train permutation and scale jointly from scratch. This is the most flexible setting. If this performs best, it suggests that scaling helps the optimizer find a lower-barrier representative from the outset, rather than merely refining an already good permutation.

\paragraph{perm\_then\_joint\_scale}
First optimize permutation only. Then continue optimization with both permutation and scale active. This tests whether a stable permutation should first be established before scale is introduced.

\paragraph{perm\_then\_scale\_only}
First optimize permutation only. Then freeze the permutation and optimize only the scale variables. This isolates the \emph{marginal value of scale} after correspondence has already been fixed.

\subsection{Why These Four Modes Are Useful}

These four modes disentangle several distinct questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{permutation\_only vs joint\_scale}: is scale helpful at all when allowed from the start?
    \item \textbf{permutation\_only vs perm\_then\_scale\_only}: can scale improve a fixed permutation?
    \item \textbf{joint\_scale vs perm\_then\_joint\_scale}: is staged optimization more stable than joint-from-scratch optimization?
    \item \textbf{perm\_then\_joint\_scale vs perm\_then\_scale\_only}: after warm start, does permutation still need to move, or is scale refinement alone sufficient?
\end{itemize}

\section{XOR Experiments}
The XOR experiments serve as the minimal testbed for separating \emph{symmetry} from \emph{search}. In the previous chapter, XOR already showed that permutation-only alignment is limited in narrow networks and improves with width. However, on those experiments alone it is unclear whether adding scale would help because it genuinely captures an additional symmetry, or simply because it makes optimization easier. XOR provides a setting where this distinction can be tested exactly, since exhaustive search over all permutations is feasible.

\subsection{Methodology}

XOR is studied with small ReLU MLPs of architecture \(2\)-\(h\)-\(1\), where \(h \in \{3,5,7\}\). The number of trained networks was not kept fixed across widths: for wider settings fewer networks, and therefore fewer model pairs, were used to keep the total experiment time manageable. All models were trained on the full XOR dataset, and only solutions that achieve \(100\%\) training accuracy and sufficiently low endpoint loss were retained. This yields \(21\) valid model pairs for \(h=3\), \(36\) valid pairs for \(h=5\), and \(10\) valid pairs for \(h=7\).

For each pair, linear interpolation in parameter space is evaluated under five alignment modes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{No Alignment}: direct interpolation between the two trained endpoints.
    \item \textbf{Sinkhorn Permutation Only (From Scratch)}: a relaxed permutation is optimized from scratch with Sinkhorn and then projected to a hard permutation.
    \item \textbf{Best Exhaustive Permutation}: all hidden-unit permutations are enumerated and the one with the lowest barrier is selected. This is the oracle best result achievable by permutation alone.
    \item \textbf{Sinkhorn Permutation + Scale (From Scratch)}: permutation and positive hidden-unit scalings are optimized jointly from scratch.
    \item \textbf{Best Exhaustive Permutation + Scale Refinement}: first find the oracle best permutation by exhaustive search, then optimize only the scale variables while keeping that permutation fixed.
\end{enumerate}

The purpose of these modes is to separate two effects. The first is whether a practical relaxed method such as Sinkhorn can \emph{find} the right correspondence between hidden units. The second, and more important, is whether positive scaling gives a genuine improvement \emph{even after the best possible permutation has already been found}. If scale refinement improves the exhaustive-permutation baseline, then the benefit cannot be attributed merely to a better search procedure; it must come from the larger symmetry class itself.

For the optimization-based methods, a default hyperparameter configuration is run first. If that default run fails to satisfy the target criterion, a bounded hyperparameter search is triggered. For the scale-based methods, the target is a small barrier threshold (\(\epsilon = 0.01\)). For Sinkhorn permutation-only, the target is the barrier achieved by the best exhaustive permutation for that same pair. The main evaluation metric is the interpolation loss barrier.

This is consistent with the earlier XOR findings in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}: wider networks provide more redundancy and therefore admit better symmetry-based alignment.

The key comparison is between \emph{Best Exhaustive Permutation} and \emph{Best Exhaustive Permutation + Scale Refinement}. Once the best permutation is already fixed, adding scale still reduces the mean barrier from \(0.357\) to \(0.247\) for \(h=3\), from \(0.111\) to \(0.025\) for \(h=5\), and from \(0.023\) to \(0.005\) for \(h=7\). This is strong evidence that scale is not merely helping the search procedure; it improves the best barrier achievable by permutation alone.

The from-scratch \emph{Sinkhorn Permutation + Scale} variant also benefits from scale, especially for \(h=5\) and \(h=7\), where it outperforms \emph{Best Exhaustive Permutation}. This indicates that, at sufficient width, a practical relaxed method can exploit the enlarged symmetry class effectively even without oracle knowledge.

\section{Sinkhorn Permutation + Scale on VGGs}

The VGG experiments serve as the large-scale counterpart to the XOR study. On XOR, exhaustive search made it possible to separate the effect of permutation from the effect of scaling exactly. On CIFAR-10 with VGG architectures, exhaustive search is infeasible, so the question becomes whether the same qualitative pattern persists in a realistic high-dimensional setting. In particular, the aim is to test whether adding scale to Sinkhorn-based alignment reduces the interpolation barrier relative to permutation-only alignment, and whether further improvement can be obtained by fixing an already found permutation and optimizing only scale on top of it.

This experiment is a direct continuation of the earlier VGG analysis in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}. There, permutation-based alignment improved connectivity but did not achieve full LMC, especially for larger distances between independently trained solutions. The XOR results of the present chapter suggest one possible explanation: permutation may be only part of the relevant symmetry. The VGG experiments therefore test whether the same conclusion extends beyond the minimal synthetic setting.

\subsection{Methodology}

The experiment compares three alignment settings on CIFAR-10 for fixed endpoint pairs of VGG11, VGG13, VGG16, and VGG19:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sinkhorn Permutation Only}: Sinkhorn permutation parameters are optimized from scratch and then projected to a hard permutation for evaluation.
    \item \textbf{Sinkhorn Permutation + Scale (Joint)}: permutation and positive hidden-channel scaling are optimized jointly from scratch. During training, the scale variables are optimized jointly with the soft Sinkhorn permutation; evaluation is performed after projecting the permutation to a hard matching.
    \item \textbf{Permutation Then Scale Finetuning}: the permutation found by the permutation-only run is fixed as a hard permutation, and only the scale variables are optimized afterward.
\end{itemize}

In all cases, the aligned model is compared to the second endpoint by evaluating test loss along the linear interpolation path in parameter space, and the loss barrier is used as the main metric.

The results do not show uniform dominance of one scale-based variant over the other. For VGG13 and VGG16, optimizing scale after first fixing the permutation yields a lower barrier than joint permutation+scale optimization, whereas for VGG11 and VGG19 the jointly optimized variant performs better. Permutation symmetry captures an important part of the geometry relevant for linear mode connectivity, but it does not appear to be the full story. Positive scaling provides an additional symmetry direction that can systematically reduce interpolation barriers. This does not prove that scaling is sufficient to recover full linear connectivity in realistic architectures, but it does show that restricting alignment to permutations alone is unnecessarily narrow.

\section{Implications for the One-Basin Modulo Permutation Hypothesis}

The experiments in this chapter also refine the interpretation of the original hypothesis. The \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis} asks whether independently trained networks become linearly connected after accounting for permutation symmetry. The present results suggest that this formulation may be too restrictive. In practice, two networks may fail to exhibit LMC not because they lie in genuinely different basins, but because permutation alone does not account for all function-preserving transformations that matter for interpolation geometry.

A more general view suggested by these results is that linear connectivity should perhaps be studied modulo a broader symmetry group that includes both permutation and scaling. Under this interpretation, the relevant question becomes not whether SGD solutions are linearly connected after permutation, but whether they are linearly connected after choosing appropriate representatives within their full symmetry class.

The present thesis does not resolve that broader question fully. However, it provides evidence for two important claims:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item failure of permutation-only alignment should not automatically be interpreted as evidence against a single-basin view, and
    \item scale symmetry can produce systematic improvements beyond the best permutation alone, both in a controlled minimal setting and in realistic VGG experiments.
\end{enumerate}

These observations strengthen the conclusion of RQ2: when alignment methods fail, this failure may reflect not only optimization limitations, but also an incomplete choice of symmetry class.

\textbf{Excluded Experiments}\\
The following experiments were conducted during the research but excluded from the main thesis as they did not directly contribute to testing the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis}. They are documented here for completeness.

\textbf{Initialization Strategy Robustness}

We tested various initialization strategies for Bézier curve training, including biased linear initialization, perturbed midpoint, and sphere-constrained initialization. All strategies converged to low-loss paths with similar barrier heights, indicating that the optimization landscape for path-finding is favorable and the results are not sensitive to initialization choices.

\textbf{Prediction Changes Along Low-Loss Paths}

We tracked how individual datapoint predictions change along trained Bézier curves connecting two modes. While the overall accuracy remains high throughout the path, individual predictions can flip multiple times, suggesting that the path traverses regions with different decision boundaries despite maintaining low loss.

\textbf{Effect of Regularization on Path Properties}

We investigated how different regularization strengths during endpoint training affect the properties of connecting Bézier curves. Stronger regularization produced curves that have less bend and whose points lie closer to the origin.

\textbf{Layer-wise Distance Along Interpolation}

We measured the $\ell_2$ distance between interpolated weights and each endpoint on a per-layer basis along Bézier curves. The analysis revealed that different layers contribute unequally to the overall weight-space distance, with early layers typically showing larger relative changes along the path.
