\section{Motivation}

Stochastic gradient descent finds solutions to neural network training, but the nature of these solutions remains poorly understood. Networks trained from different initializations converge to different points in parameter space. Do these points lie in isolated basins, separated by high-loss barriers? Or do they all belong to a single connected region of low loss?


\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{example_loss_landscapev2.png}
    \caption{Example loss landscape in parameter space: optimization trajectories from different random initializations converge to distinct local minima}
    \label{fig:placeholder}
\end{figure}

Multiple isolated basins would mean that initialization is critical and that independently trained models occupy genuinely different, separated regions of the loss landscape. A single basin would mean that distinct solutions are fundamentally related, with their apparent diversity arising from symmetries in the parameterization.

This question has practical implications. If solutions lie in the same basin, they can be connected by linear paths along which the loss remains low. This enables \textbf{model merging}: combining separately trained models by averaging their weights, which is useful for federated learning where data cannot be centralized. It also enables \textbf{efficient ensembling}: sampling diverse models along low-loss paths rather than training multiple networks from scratch. Conversely, if solutions lie in isolated basins, linear interpolation between them crosses high-loss barriers, and weight averaging produces dysfunctional networks. Understanding the basin structure is therefore essential for determining when these practical techniques can succeed, while also providing valuable insight into loss landscape geometry.

\section{Problem Statement}

\citep{entezari2021role} conjectured that most SGD solutions can be permuted so that there is no barrier on the linear interpolation between them---the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis}. Formally:

\begin{quote}
\textit{For most pairs of SGD solutions $\theta_A$ and $\theta_B$, there exists a permutation $P$ such that the loss barrier on the linear path between $\theta_A$ and $P(\theta_B)$ is approximately zero.}
\end{quote}

This conjecture suggests that the loss landscape may be fundamentally simpler than it appears, with apparently distinct basins being permutations of the same underlying solution. However, the conjecture remains unproven, and existing empirical evidence is inconclusive. Moreover, the statement is inherently ambiguous—"most" is undefined, and there is no threshold specified for what constitutes "approximately zero" barrier.

The central question this thesis addresses is:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Do independently trained neural networks lie in the same basin of the loss landscape if permutation symmetries are taken into account?}
\end{quote}

\section{Limitations of Existing Evidence}

Current empirical tests of the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesi} face several limitations. 
First, successful demonstrations of linear mode connectivity have been restricted 
to specific architecture–dataset combinations. When alignment methods are applied 
to more challenging architectures such as VGG16 on CIFAR-10, they fail to achieve 
full linear mode connectivity—even when the networks achieve similar, high test 
accuracy and agree on the vast majority of predictions \citep{ainsworth2023git}, indicating highly similar predictive behavior. This raises a fundamental question:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Do alignment methods fail because they are unable to find the correct permutation within a vast combinatorial search space?
    \item Or do multiple basins genuinely exist, meaning that no permutation achieving linear mode connectivity exists for those models?
\end{itemize}

The difficulty of resolving this question theoretically comes from the intrinsic 
complexity of modern neural networks. Their loss landscapes are high-dimensional, 
non-convex, and exhibit symmetries due to parameter permutations and 
scaling invariances. As a result, rigorous global statements about basin structure 
are notoriously challenging to establish.

To conclusively prove or disprove the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis}, 
one would need to either:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Develop a robust and principled permutation-alignment method that consistently achieves
    linear mode connectivity across architectures and datasets, demonstrating that apparent
    barriers arise solely from misalignment in parameter space rather than from genuinely distinct basins, or
    \item Prove that for at least one non-degenerate architecture–dataset configuration, 
    no permutation within the full symmetry group yields linear mode connectivity, 
    thereby establishing the existence of genuinely distinct basins.
\end{itemize}

\section{Research Questions}

This thesis investigates the validity of the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis} through the following research questions:

\begin{enumerate}  
    \item[\textbf{RQ1}] \textbf{(Hypothesis Validity)} Do any two independently trained neural networks lie in the same basin modulo permutation?

    \item[\textbf{RQ2}] \textbf{(Method Limitations)} When alignment methods fail to achieve linear mode connectivity, is this due to limitations of the methods or evidence against the hypothesis?
\end{enumerate}
\noindent We assume throughout that networks share the same architecture, training set, loss function, and hyperparameters---differing only in random initialization and batch ordering. The specific architectures, datasets, and experimental configurations are detailed in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}.


\section{Contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Controlled Permutation Benchmark:} A methodology to validate permutation-finding methods by constructing pairs of networks where ground truth permutation is known by construction. This distinguishes method failure from hypothesis failure.
    
    \item \textbf{Same-Function Test (Pure XOR):} An experiment testing whether networks that compute exactly the same function (100\% accuracy on XOR) are linearly connected modulo permutation---a necessary condition for the hypothesis.
    

    \item \textbf{Distance-Connectivity Analysis:} Systematic study of how the distance between solutions in parameter space affects alignment quality and barrier height.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Definitions and Theoretical Foundations}
\label{sec:definitions} 
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Loss Landscapes}

The loss landscape of a neural network is the function $\mathcal{L}: \Theta \to \mathbb{R}$ mapping parameters $\theta \in \Theta$ to loss. Understanding the geometry of this landscape---particularly the structure of minima and the paths connecting them---is fundamental to understanding why neural network optimization succeeds.

\subsection{Basin}

A basin in the loss landscape is informally a connected region of parameter space containing a local minimum, where gradient-based optimization initialized within this region will converge to that minimum. More precisely, for a minimum $\theta^*$, its basin is the set of parameter initializations from which gradient descent converges to $\theta^*$.

For the purposes of mode connectivity, we use a relaxed definition: two solutions $\theta_A$ and $\theta_B$ are in the same basin if they can be connected by a continuous path along which the loss remains low (close to the endpoint losses). This definition focuses on the connectivity structure rather than the convergence behavior.


\subsection{Loss Barrier}

The loss barrier \citep{frankle2020linear} quantifies the difficulty of linearly interpolating between two solutions. Given two parameter vectors $\theta_A$ and $\theta_B$ with comparable loss:

\begin{equation}
\text{barrier}(\theta_A, \theta_B) = \max_{\lambda \in [0,1]} \mathcal{L}\!\left((1-\lambda)\theta_A + \lambda \theta_B\right)
 - \frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{L}(\theta_A) + \mathcal{L}(\theta_B)\right)
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{L}$ is the loss function (e.g., cross-entropy). The barrier is non-negative by definition and is typically approximated by evaluating the loss at discrete interpolation points (e.g., $\lambda = 0.0, 0.1, \ldots, 1.0$).

\textbf{Interpretation:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Barrier $\approx 0$: Flat or convex interpolation; solutions are linearly connected.
    \item Barrier $> 0$: A loss ``hill'' exists between solutions.
    \item Barrier $\approx$ loss at random initialization: Solutions are effectively disconnected.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{imabarrier_example_v3ge.png}
    \caption{Loss along the interpolation between two modes showing different barrier regimes.}
    \label{fig:placeholder}
\end{figure}
\subsection{Mode Connectivity}

Two solutions are \textbf{mode connected} if there exists a continuous path $\gamma : [0,1] \to \Theta$ with $\gamma(0)=\theta_A$ and $\gamma(1)=\theta_B$ such that:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\gamma(t)) \approx \mathcal{L}(\theta_A) \approx \mathcal{L}(\theta_B) \quad \forall\, t \in [0,1]
\end{equation}

This definition allows curved paths (Bézier curves, polygonal chains), which is less restrictive than linear mode connectivity.


\subsection{Linear Mode Connectivity}

Two networks $\theta_A$ and $\theta_B$ are \textbf{linearly mode connected} (LMC) if the loss along the linear interpolation $\theta_\lambda = (1-\lambda)\theta_A + \lambda\theta_B$ remains low for all $\lambda \in [0,1]$. The literature does not prescribe a universal threshold for ``low.'' In practice, LMC is typically considered to hold when the interpolated model remains functional---e.g., when test accuracy at any point along the interpolation stays within a few percentage points of the endpoint accuracies \citep{ainsworth2023git}, or equivalently, when the loss barrier is negligible compared to the loss at random initialization.

\subsection{Permutation Symmetry}

Neural networks admit many parameterizations that represent exactly the same function. The symmetry most relevant for this thesis is permutation symmetry: hidden units or channels may be reordered, as long as the next layer is adjusted accordingly \citep{hechtnielsen1990algebraic}. The same idea also appears in other architectures, for example in attention-based models where heads can often be permuted without changing the represented function. For the purposes of this thesis, however, it is sufficient to consider fully connected hidden layers and convolutional layers.

\paragraph{Hidden layers.}
For two consecutive fully connected layers,
\[
f(x) = W_2 \,\sigma(W_1 x),
\]
let $P$ be a permutation matrix acting on the hidden units. If the first layer is permuted on its output side and the second layer is compensated on its input side, then the function is unchanged:
\[
\widetilde{f}(x)
=
(W_2 P^\top)\,\sigma(P W_1 x)
=
W_2 P^\top P \,\sigma(W_1 x)
=
W_2 \sigma(W_1 x)
=
f(x).
\]
Thus, reordering hidden neurons and undoing the same reordering in the next layer leaves the network invariant. The same idea extends directly to deeper fully connected networks. If a network has weight matrices \(W_1,\dots,W_L\), and if each hidden layer \(\ell\) is permuted by a permutation matrix \(P_\ell\), then the transformed weights are
\[
W_1' = P_1 W_1,\qquad
W_\ell' = P_\ell W_\ell P_{\ell-1}^{\top}\;(\ell=2,\dots,L-1),\qquad
W_L' = W_L P_{L-1}^{\top}.
\]
Here, left multiplication by \(P_\ell\) permutes the output coordinates of layer \(\ell\), while right multiplication by \(P_{\ell-1}^{\top}\) compensates for the permutation of the previous hidden layer on the input side. The first layer has no preceding hidden layer, so only left multiplication appears, while the output layer has no following hidden layer, so only the right-side compensation remains.

\paragraph{Convolutional layers.}
The same argument holds for convolutional channels. Let the first convolution produce hidden feature maps
\[
h = \sigma(W_1 * x),
\]
and let $P$ permute the output channels of this layer. Then the permuted hidden representation is
\[
\widetilde{h} = \sigma((P W_1) * x) = P h,
\]
and the next convolution can compensate by permuting its input-channel dimension with $P^\top$:
\[
(W_2 P^\top) * \widetilde{h}
=
(W_2 P^\top) * (P h)
=
W_2 * h.
\]
Therefore, permuting the channels of one convolutional layer and applying the inverse permutation to the input channels of the next layer does not change the network function. This extends in the same way to deeper convolutional networks, with exactly the same algebraic form as above. The only difference is how the multiplications are interpreted: for convolutional kernels, the left multiplication acts on the output-channel axis and the right multiplication acts on the input-channel axis. The reason the same formula applies is that a convolution is still linear in its channel coordinates: reordering the output channels of one layer simply reorders the feature maps passed to the next layer, so the next layer can undo this exactly by applying the inverse permutation on its input-channel dimension.

In both cases, permutations can be chosen independently at each hidden layer or hidden channel dimension. This creates a large family of parameter vectors that are functionally identical, even though they may be far apart in Euclidean parameter space.

\subsection{The One-Basin Modulo Permutation Hypothesis}

\citep{entezari2021role} conjectured that most SGD solutions belong to a set whose elements can be permuted so that linear interpolation between any two permuted elements has low barrier---the one-basin modulo permtuation hypothesis. Formally, for most pairs of SGD solutions $\theta_A, \theta_B$:
\begin{equation}
\exists\, P \in \mathcal{P} \text{ such that } \theta_A \text{ and } P(\theta_B) \text{ are linearly mode connected.}
\end{equation}
This accounts for the fact that $\theta_B$ and $P(\theta_B)$ compute the same function but occupy different locations in parameter space due to permutation symmetry. Importantly, even if $\theta_A$ and $P(\theta_B)$ are LMC, they may still compute different functions---the hypothesis claims only that they lie in the same low-loss basin, not that all SGD solutions are functionally equivalent.

\subsection{Hierarchy of Connectivity Claims}

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Hierarchy of connectivity claims, from weakest to strongest.}
    \label{tab:hierarchy}
    \begin{tabular}{l l l}

The \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis} corresponds to ``LMC modulo permutation''---stronger than general mode connectivity but weaker than claiming raw linear connectivity.

\section{Evidence For and Against the One-Basin Modulo Permutation Hypothesis}
\label{sec:literature}
%==============================================================================

This section traces the development of mode connectivity research, from early results establishing curved-path connectivity through modern permutation-finding algorithms. Each result is contextualized by its contribution to understanding the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis}.

\subsection{Mode Connectivity via Curved Paths}

The study of mode connectivity began with the discovery that trained networks can be connected by low-loss \textit{curved} paths, even when linear interpolation fails.

\citet{draxler2018essentially} used the Nudged Elastic Band (NEB) algorithm from computational chemistry to find minimum-energy paths between networks trained on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100. Their key finding: ``minima are perhaps best seen as points on a single connected manifold of low loss, rather than as the bottoms of distinct valleys.''

\citet{garipov2018loss} showed that simple parametric curves suffice to connect minima. They proposed two approaches:

\textit{Quadratic Bézier curve:}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bezier}
\theta(t) = (1-t)^2 \theta_A + 2t(1-t)\theta_{\text{mid}} + t^2 \theta_B
\end{equation}

\textit{Polygonal chain:}
\begin{equation}
\theta(t) = \begin{cases}
2(t\theta_{\text{mid}} + (0.5 - t)\theta_A) & 0 \leq t \leq 0.5 \\
2((t - 0.5)\theta_B + (1 - t)\theta_{\text{mid}}) & 0.5 \leq t \leq 1
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $\theta_{\text{mid}}$ is a trainable midpoint optimized to minimize the expected loss along the path.

These results establish that minima are connected, suggesting the loss landscape has a simpler structure than previously thought. However, the existence of curved paths does not imply LMC---even a single bend point means the path deviates from the direct linear interpolation, leaving open whether linear connectivity could be achieved without such detours.



\subsection{The Entezari Conjecture and Statistical Evidence}

\citep{entezari2021role} proposed the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis} and provided indirect statistical evidence. Their key observation: the distribution of barriers between independently trained networks is statistically indistinguishable from the distribution of barriers between random permutations of the \textit{same} network.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{enzari.png}
    \caption{\textbf{Left:} Permuting hidden units of networks B, C, D to B', C', D' may eliminate barriers with A while preserving function. \textbf{Middle:} The hypothesis models independently trained networks as random permutations of the same underlying solution. \textbf{Right:} Barrier distributions between independent networks match those between random permutations of the same network across 3000+ networks. Points in the lower left mostly correspond to lowest barrier found after searching in the space of valid permutations using a Simulated Annealing algorithm.}
    \label{fig:entezari}
\end{figure}

The interpretation: if independently trained networks ``look like'' permuted versions of each other in terms of barrier statistics, they may actually \textit{be} permuted versions of the same underlying solution. This provides the first empirical motivation for the conjecture. However, the evidence is \textit{indirect}---the authors did not find permutations that actually achieve low barriers; they only showed that barrier distributions match. The evidence is insufficient, rather unmatching distributions of barrier would be a more convincing evidence of the incorrectness of the hypothesis. The hypothesis remained unverified: could such permutations actually be found?

\section{Mode Connectivity via Curved Paths}

First, we compare low-loss nonlinear connectivity in two settings that are central to the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation} hypothesis. We consider pairs of independently trained models initialized from different random seeds. Second, we consider a model together with a permuted version of itself. This mirrors the comparison made by \citep{entezari2021role} in Figure~\ref{fig:entezari}, who studied barrier distributions for independently trained networks and for networks matched by permutation. Here, instead of focusing only on linear interpolation, we ask whether the same two scenarios also exhibit similarly low barriers along learned nonlinear paths. If they do not, that would suggest that the two cases may not be geometrically equivalent, despite the permutation-based hypothesis.

To test this, we train Bézier curves in two settings: (1) between independently initialized models (`seed0--seed1`), and (2) between a model and a permuted version of itself (`seed0--mirror`). Since low-loss curved paths are already known to exist between independently trained models \citep{garipov2018loss}, one would expect such paths to exist at least as readily between a model and a permuted copy, because the two endpoints implement the same function and differ only by a hidden-unit permutation. 

\subsection{Methodology}

Quadratic Bézier curves are trained with fixed endpoints and a single trainable interior bend, corresponding to the parameterization in equation~\eqref{eq:bezier}. Across the compared settings, the curve-training setup is kept the same so that any difference in the resulting path quality is attributable to the relation between the endpoints rather than to a different optimization schedule. Full hyperparameter details are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:curved_path_repro}.

For the mirror setting, the second endpoint is constructed by reversing the order of hidden units within each hidden layer:
\[
\bigl(P_\ell^{\mathrm{mirror}}\bigr)_{ij} =
\begin{cases}
1, & j = n_\ell + 1 - i, \\
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\]
where $n_\ell$ is the width of hidden layer $\ell$. Thus, unit \(i\) is mapped to position \(n_\ell+1-i\). The same construction applies to convolutional layers, where it reverses the order of output channels. For the random-permutation setting, the second endpoint is obtained instead by applying a full-network random permutation, while keeping the represented function unchanged.

\subsection{Results}


\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{combined_connectivity.png}
    \caption{Linear and curve-based connectivity for VGG16 on CIFAR10. The main panels show loss and error along the full interpolation path. The insets show the corresponding curve-only trajectories, that is, the same quantities without linear interpolation, in order to make the differences between seed-seed and seed-mirror pairs more visible in the low-barrier regime.}
    \label{fig:combined_connectivity}
\end{figure}
The main panels of Figure~\ref{fig:combined_connectivity} show that both settings admit low-loss curved paths, with barriers below roughly $2.5\%$ test error and below $0.06$ test loss. This confirms that curved mode connectivity is present in our setup, since such barrier values still correspond to functional models along the path. At the same time, the insets reveal a systematic difference: the barriers for mirror-permuted pairs are consistently higher than those for independently trained pairs. This is notable because the mirror endpoints are functionally identical, whereas independently trained models need not be. To test whether this effect is specific to the mirror construction or reflects a more general property of permutation-related pairs, we next repeat the experiment with random permutations instead of deterministic mirroring.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{randpermute.png}
\caption{Mode connectivity between three independently trained networks and between the same networks and their random permutations (without linear interpolation for clarity).}
\label{fig:baseline-bezier-nointerpolation_randpermute}
\end{figure}



As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:baseline-bezier-nointerpolation_randpermute}, random permutations yield low-loss curves that are virtually indistinguishable from those obtained between independently trained models. The loss profiles along the optimized Bézier paths exhibit no pronounced peak and closely match both the shape and magnitude observed in the independent-seed setting. This shows that the elevated barrier observed for mirror permutations is not a generic consequence of permutation symmetry, but rather a feature of that particular construction. Why the mirror permutation behaves differently from a random permutation is not clear, but resolving that question is not important for the present thesis. What matters here is that curved low-loss paths remain robust both between independent solutions and across random permutation symmetries.

\section{Hyperplane-Constrained Path Finding}
The Bézier experiments above show that low-loss curved paths can be found both between independently trained models and between models related by permutation. This raises a more geometric question: how much freedom is actually needed to find such paths? If low-loss paths are rare and occupy only narrow corridors in parameter space, then even a simple geometric constraint should make path optimization much harder. Conversely, if low-loss paths are abundant, then imposing a weak constraint should have little effect. To test this, we constrained the trainable midpoint parameter vector of a three-bend polygonal chain to lie in random affine subspaces that pass through the midpoint of the linear interpolation. Importantly, the codimension constraint is applied only to this trainable point, not to the entire path.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{canvaxd_v2.png}
    \caption{Schematic of path optimization under a hyperplane constraint. The midpoint between two solutions is initialized on the linear interpolation and restricted to move inside a fixed hyperplane. A path is then obtained by connecting the endpoints through the optimized midpoint. The constraining hyperplane is not assumed to be the perpendicular bisector of the linear interpolation between the endpoints.}
    \label{fig:hyperplane_constraint_schematic}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Methodology}

For two endpoints $w_0$ and $w_1$, let
\[
\mathbf{m}=\frac{1}{2}(w_0+w_1).
\]
The polygonal path is then defined by connecting $w_0$ to a trainable midpoint $\theta$ and $\theta$ to $w_1$, while requiring only this midpoint $\theta$ to remain inside a randomly chosen subspace through $\mathbf{m}$. The two line segments joining $w_0$ to $\theta$ and $\theta$ to $w_1$ are not themselves separately constrained. In the implementation, the midpoint is flattened into a single parameter vector, and the constraint is written as
\[
N^\top(\theta-\mathbf{m})=0,
\]
where \(N=[n_1,\dots,n_k]\) contains \(k\) orthonormal random directions in the full parameter space, and \(k\) is the codimension, so the feasible set is the intersection of the \(k\) random hyperplanes
\[
n_i^\top(\theta-\mathbf{m})=0,\qquad i=1,\dots,k.
\]
The random directions are sampled from a standard Gaussian distribution and orthonormalized by Gram--Schmidt. After each gradient step, the trainable midpoint is projected back onto this intersection by removing its components along the constrained directions:
\[
\theta_{\mathrm{proj}}
=
\theta-\sum_{i=1}^k \bigl((\theta-\mathbf{m})^\top n_i\bigr)n_i.
\]
Thus, optimization is free to move inside the \((N-k)\)-dimensional affine subspace orthogonal to the sampled directions, but not along those directions themselves. We varied the codimension \(k\), using \(k\in\{1,5,10,30\}\).



\subsection{Results}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{random_plane_midpoint_codim1_5_10_30_seed0-seed1.png}
    \caption{Comparison of polygonal path optimization when the trainable midpoint is constrained to random subspaces through the interpolation midpoint, for codimensions $1$, $5$, $10$, and $30$.}
    \label{fig:random_planes_comparison}
\end{figure}


The result is that all tested codimensions yield virtually identical low-loss paths: across this range of codimensions, the differences are negligible. At the same time, the optimized trainable midpoints found by the constrained searches are different, showing that multiple distinct constrained solutions can yield similarly good low-loss connections.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \label{tab:random_plane_codim_results}
    \begin{tabular}{ccc}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Codimension} & \textbf{Max Test Error} & \textbf{Barrier Height} \\
        \midrule
        1 & 8.19\% & 1.23\% \\
        5 & 8.24\% & 1.27\% \\
        10 & 8.11\% & 1.15\% \\
        30 & 8.03\% & 1.07\% \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Path quality under random hyperplane constraints of different codimensions.}
\end{table}

The interpretation is that constraining a single trainable point by these codimension conditions remains extremely weak relative to the ambient parameter dimension of roughly $15$ million. Even after removing up to $30$ directions from the admissible location of the midpoint, the optimizer still has enough freedom to find essentially the same quality of low-loss connection. The important point is therefore not that a particular constrained subspace is special, but that the low-loss region is broad enough to admit many different constrained connecting points.

This supports a broader picture in which trained solutions are not connected only by delicate, low-dimensional corridors. Instead, the low-loss region appears thick enough that even constrained path searches can reach different trainable points while retaining essentially the same path quality. A natural next question is how far this can be pushed: at what codimension does connectivity begin to degrade? Systematically increasing the codimension would provide a more direct way to estimate the effective thickness of the low-loss region.

    


\section{Controlled LMC Benchmark with Known Permutations}
\label{sec:controlled_benchmark}

In contrast to the Bézier experiments above, which test the existence of curved low-loss paths (mode connectivity), the following experiment focuses on linear mode connectivity (LMC) via straight-line interpolation. LMC is a strictly stronger geometric condition than curved connectivity: failure of LMC does not imply disconnection, but rather linear separation.

The goal of this experiment is to evaluate whether permutation-finding methods succeed when ground truth is available. Prior work has not evaluated alignment methods against known ground-truth permutations. Existing evaluations typically measure only the final barrier after alignment; if the barrier remains high, it is unclear whether (a) the method failed to recover a good permutation that exists, or (b) no such permutation exists, meaning the networks genuinely lie in different basins.

Importantly, prior work \citep{entezari2021role} focuses on aligning a model with a permuted version of itself—i.e., recovering the exact symmetry that makes two networks functionally identical. In that setting, successful alignment reduces one network to the other up to permutation. Our objective is different: we seek a permutation that aligns two \emph{distinct trained modes} so that they become linearly mode connected, not necessarily identical. The task is therefore not to reconstruct an exact symmetry transformation, but to find a permutation that reduces the LMC barrier between genuinely different solutions.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{construction.png}
    \caption{Starting from a shared initialization $x_{\mathrm{init}}$, training splits at $x_{\mathrm{split}}$ to produce two related modes $x_0$ and $x_1$. A known permutation maps $x_1 \to x_1'$, and weight matching attempts to recover $P(x_1')$. The blue and green dotted lines indicate the distances between modes, as referred to in the later part of the experiment.}
    \label{fig:placeholder}
\end{figure}

To create a controlled benchmark, we apply a known permutation $P^*$ to one network in a pair that is already LMC-connected, and test whether the alignment method can recover connectivity. Because a correct permutation is guaranteed to exist, success validates the method’s ability to recover meaningful alignments. Conversely, failure on this controlled task would indicate that the method is too weak to draw conclusions about the hypothesis from its performance. We adopted the shared initialization with split training approach from \citep{frankle2020linear} to construct these network pairs and weight matching \citep{ainsworth2023git} as a permutation finding method.

\subsection{Methodology}

We fix $N = 200$ total epochs (the number required for this model to converge) and vary $n$ (the split point) to control the distance between $w_0$ and $w_1$. Longer shared training (larger $n$) produces closer networks while shorter shared training makes them more distant.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Train a model for $n$ epochs with a fixed batch ordering, producing a shared checkpoint $w_{\mathrm{shared}}$.
    \item Create two copies of $w_{\mathrm{shared}}$ and continue training them independently for $N-n$ epochs using different batch orderings. This produces two related endpoints, $w_0$ and $w_1$. \bluecircle{}
    \item Apply a known random permutation $P^\ast$ to one endpoint, giving $w_1' = P^\ast(w_1)$.
    \item Run weight matching between $w_0$ and the permuted endpoint $w_1'$ to recover alignment $P$.
    \item Test whether the recovered alignment removes the artificial permutation by comparing the original $\operatorname{barrier}(w_0, w_1)$ with the aligned $\operatorname{barrier}\bigl(w_0, P(w_1')\bigr)$. \greencircle{}
\end{enumerate}
Full training and evaluation details are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:controlled_xor_repro}.



\subsection{Results}
In every case the recovered $\text{barrier}(w_0, P(w_1'))$ is lower than the original $\text{barrier}(w_0, w_1)$, suggesting the alignment method finds permutations that improves connectivity beyond the ground truth. This confirms that the method works correctly and manages to find an optimal permutation, at least when one exists.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{train_accuracy.png}
    \caption{
    Train barrier as a function of $\ell_2$ distance between weight vectors, before and after permutation alignment. Blue and green circles correspond to distances and barriers between $x_0$ and $x_1$, and between $x_0$ and $P(x_1')$, respectively; red diamonds denote independently trained networks.}
    \label{fig:barrier-vs-distance}
\end{figure}


The important observation is that weight matching is always applied to the pair $(w_0,w_1')$, where $w_1' = P^\ast(w_1)$ is obtained by applying a random permutation to $w_1$. Because of this artificial permutation, the apparent parameter-space mismatch between $w_0$ and $w_1'$ is large in all split settings, and is comparable to the mismatch between independently initialized models. Nevertheless, the quality of the recovered alignment depends strongly on how the underlying pair $(w_0,w_1)$ was created. When the two models share most of their training trajectory, alignment recovers a low-barrier connection. When the split occurs earlier and the models train independently for longer, the post-alignment barrier remains much larger. Thus, even though weight matching sees a similarly difficult-looking permuted pair in all cases, it succeeds only when the underlying unpermuted endpoints are already close.

This is precisely why the controlled benchmark is useful. For each shared-training split, we know that the artificial permutation can be undone: applying $(P^\ast)^{-1}$ to $w_1'$ recovers the original endpoint $w_1$. Therefore, each run comes with a guaranteed reference barrier, namely the barrier of the constructed pair $(w_0,w_1)$ before the random permutation was applied. The five runs differ only in how far apart the underlying endpoints are, controlled by the amount of shared training. After the artificial permutation, however, all runs present weight matching with the same kind of task: align $w_0$ to a randomly permuted version of another trained model. If permutation symmetry were sufficient to recover LMC between arbitrary modes, one might expect these controlled cases to yield comparable results after alignment, since each contains, in theory, a permutation that cancels out the permutation responsible for breaking LMC. Instead, weight matching succeeds only in cases where we know such an optimal permutation exists by construction, but not in cases where this is unknown; this raises the question of whether such permutations exist in the latter setting at all.

Moreover, we found that at distance $\approx 35$ corresponding to the $8/192$ split, the models are only weakly LMC-connected, with barriers of around 20\% accuracy error. Thus, even for shared-initialization pairs linear mode connectivity degrades substantially at large parameter distances. This reflects an intrinsic limitation of LMC at large separations in parameter space. In other words, LMC after permutation does not hold because the recovered permutation leaves the aligned models too far apart. If an optimal permutation truly places two modes within the same linear basin, one would expect their post-alignment distance to be small, at least for a weight-matching method. The persistence of large distances after alignment therefore still suggests either that no such permutation exists, or that current alignment methods fail to recover it. This raises a natural question: if LMC already becomes fragile at this scale, can we reasonably expect permutation alignment to achieve LMC for independently initialized networks whose post-alignment distance is comparable, if we cannot even construct modes with this distance mismatch and no barrier between them?

The analysis above leaves a few possible interpretations. One is that the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis} is too strong: permutation symmetry alone may not be sufficient to make independently trained modes linearly connected, since the alignment method fails to find suitable permutations for them but succeeds when such a permutation is guaranteed to exist. Another possibility is that the constructed shared-training models with later splits are easier for weight matching to align in some implicit, non-obvious way.



\section{XOR Experiment: Validating Alignment on a Minimal Problem}

The experiment above shows that alignment performance depends strongly on the underlying distance between modes. However, it still does not fully resolve whether failures at larger distances are caused by limitations of the alignment algorithm or by the absence of any permutation that would yield LMC. To separate these possibilities, we next study the XOR problem, which provides a minimal and fully analyzable setting for permutation alignment. With a 2-2-1 architecture, there are only two hidden-unit permutations: the identity and a swap. Exhaustive search is therefore trivial, allowing us to verify exactly when alignment succeeds or fails, and why.


Additionally, XOR is interesting because multiple distinct decision boundaries can achieve 100\% classification accuracy. Networks may learn different weight configurations while computing functions that agree on the four training points but differ elsewhere. This allows us to test alignment between networks with both similar and different decision boundaries.

\subsection{Methodology}

We use the 4-point XOR problem with inputs \(\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\}\) and binary labels \(\{0,1,1,0\}\), together with a 2-2-1 MLP using ReLU activations and a single logit output. Independently initialized networks are trained until they solve the task, yielding a collection of endpoint pairs.

For each pair, both hidden-unit permutations are checked exhaustively: the identity and the swap. The better permutation is selected by requiring \(100\%\) interpolation accuracy and then minimizing interpolation loss. This makes the alignment search exact in the minimal-width XOR setting. Full training details are given in Appendix~\ref{app:controlled_xor_repro}.

Successful alignment occurs only in cases where networks happen to learn nearly identical weights. \section{XOR Experiment: Validating Mode Connectivity on a Minimal Problem}

The permutation-alignment experiment above showed that LMC fails after permutation allignment for most pairs in the minimal 2–2–1 XOR architecture. However, failure of linear mode connectivity does not imply the absence of curved connectivity. To determine whether the observed failures reflect genuine structural disconnection or simply the absence of LMC after permutation alignment, we next directly investigate curved mode connectivity in the same minimal setting. We attempted to find low-loss curved paths between independently trained XOR solutions using both Bézier curves and polygonal chains. Initially, we used a single trainable midpoint. When this failed to produce low-loss connections, we progressively increased the number of trainable points (up to 10), greatly increasing the flexibility of the path parameterization.

\subsection{Methodology}

The XOR dataset, model class, and endpoint-training setup are the same as in the previous XOR alignment experiment. The difference is the object being optimized: instead of searching over permutations, we directly optimize curved connecting paths between independently trained endpoints. We evaluate both Bézier curves and polygonal chains, beginning with a single trainable midpoint and then increasing the number of trainable points up to 10 when necessary to test whether additional path flexibility is sufficient to recover a low-loss connection. Full optimization details are summarized in Appendix~\ref{app:controlled_xor_repro}.

\subsection{Results}

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{unsuccesful_10-14_bezier_3bend.png}
    \caption{No low-loss path found (seeds 10→14): networks with different decision boudaries.}
    \label{fig:placeholder}
\end{figure}

Despite this, low-loss paths were found only between models that were virtually identical in their weights (i.e., trivial connections). For non-trivial pairs, no method succeeded in discovering a low-loss curved path. This held even for models whose decision boundaries appeared visually identical. Thus, in the minimal-width 2-2-1 architecture, two independently trained solutions cannot in general be connected, even nonlinearly, unless they are already nearly identical in parameter space.

These results mirror our earlier permutation-alignment experiment on the minimal XOR architecture: alignment succeeded only in degenerate cases where the networks were effectively identical up to permutation. The absence of curved connectivity here suggests that width $m=2$ provides insufficient redundancy to allow different initializations to lie within the same connected low-loss manifold.

\subsubsection{Effect of Increasing Width}

To determine at what width the solution set becomes connected, we increased the hidden layer to $m=3$ neurons (2--3--1 architecture). With only one additional hidden unit, all independently trained models were successfully connected by low-loss curved paths, regardless of differences in their internal weight configurations or decision boundaries.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{3hidden_xor_bezier.png}

Interestingly, for one seed pair (1→14), none of the path-finding methods succeeded directly, even after increasing the number of trainable points. However, we were able to find low-loss paths between 1→7 and 7→14. Since connectivity is transitive under concatenation, this implies that a low-loss path must exist between 1→14 as well. Indeed, a piecewise construction (a polygonal chain passing through the intermediate solution with 3 trainable points) would connect them by concatenating the two low-loss segments.

The failure of direct optimization in this case indicates that low-loss paths in the $m=3$ setting, while existent, may get stuck in local minima and therefore be difficult to find with a limited path parameterization. This highlights that low-loss path-finding algorithms are imperfect: failure to discover a low-loss path does not necessarily imply its non-existence, particularly in narrow networks with limited representational redundancy.



Overall, these results demonstrate a sharp transition in connectivity behavior between width $m=2$ and $m=3$. Minimal-width XOR solutions appear genuinely disconnected in parameter space (except for trivial cases), whereas adding a single hidden neuron provides enough redundancy for the solutions to become connected by curved low-loss paths.

\chapter{Beyond Permutation: Scale-Aware Alignment for Linear Mode Connectivity}
\label{chap:symmetry-aware}
%==============================================================================

The previous chapters showed that low-loss connectivity and linear mode connectivity are different problems. Curved paths were often easy to find, even when the path search was geometrically constrained, suggesting that trained solutions can belong to a broad connected low-loss region. Linear mode connectivity is stricter: it asks whether two endpoints can be connected by the straight line between their parameters. The controlled permutation benchmark showed that permutation alignment works when a good permutation is guaranteed to exist, but its performance degrades as the underlying modes move farther apart. The XOR experiments sharpened this point further: because all permutations could be checked exhaustively, we saw that even the best possible permutation does not always remove the interpolation barrier. This suggests that permutation symmetry may not capture all structure relevant for low-barrier interpolation. 

Another possibility is that permutation invariance does not capture the full symmetry structure relevant for low-barrier interpolation. Neural networks with positively homogeneous activations such as ReLU possess an additional layerwise scaling symmetry. A positively homogeneous activation satisfies \(\sigma(cz)=c\,\sigma(z)\) for all \(c>0\). Therefore, for a two-layer block
\[
f(x)=W_2\,\sigma(W_1x),
\]
one may rescale a hidden unit by a positive constant \(c\) without changing the represented function:
\[
(W_2D^{-1})\,\sigma(DW_1x)
=(W_2D^{-1})D\,\sigma(W_1x)
=W_2\,\sigma(W_1x), \qquad
D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\dots,d_m),\quad d_i>0.
\]
Thus, hidden units can be rescaled by positive constants provided the adjacent layer compensates appropriately, leaving the network function unchanged. Two networks may therefore represent similar computations while differing not only in the ordering of hidden units, but also in the internal scale assigned to those units. This motivates the main question of this chapter:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Can linear mode connectivity be improved by aligning networks modulo a larger symmetry class that includes both permutation and positive scaling?}
\end{quote}

The XOR results make this question particularly concrete. Exhaustive permutation search gave an oracle permutation baseline: for each pair, we knew the best barrier achievable by permutation alone. Whenever a nonzero barrier remained after that oracle alignment, the cause could not be a failure of permutation search. A natural next hypothesis is that part of the remaining barrier comes from scale mismatch between corresponding hidden units.  The present chapter investigates this possibility in two steps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item We first introduce a scale-aware extension of permutation alignment, in which positive scaling is added to the permutation-finding procedure.
    \item We then test this extension on both minimal XOR networks, where exhaustive search provides a permutation-only oracle baseline, and larger networks, where scale-aware alignment is evaluated as a practical re-basing method.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Choosing a Base Method for Scale-Aware Alignment}

Before introducing a scale-aware alignment method, we first compare the main re-basin methods to determine which one is best suited for jointly incorporating scale-invariance optimization into permutation finding. The goal is not to show that scaling is impossible to add to other methods, but to identify which framework admits the cleanest minimal modification. The key criterion is whether scale variables can be incorporated without substantially changing the method's original optimization structure.

\subsection{Activation Matching}
Activation matching is data-dependent: the activation matrices are obtained by running a set of inputs through both networks and recording the hidden-layer activations. It then solves a linear assignment problem using these activations. For activations \(Z^{(A)}, Z^{(B)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}\), the layer permutation is chosen as
\[
P_\ell
=
\arg\min_{P \in S_d}
\sum_{i=1}^n \|Z^{(A)}_{:,i} - P Z^{(B)}_{:,i}\|^2
=
\arg\max_{P \in S_d}
\langle P, Z^{(A)}(Z^{(B)})^\top \rangle_F.
\]
Here \(\langle A,B\rangle_F=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ij}\) denotes the Frobenius inner product. Since the objective is linear in the entries of the permutation matrix \(P\), this optimization is a standard linear assignment problem (LAP), for which efficient solvers are available.
This works cleanly when the activation similarity matrix is fixed before solving the assignment problem. Adding scale makes the activations themselves depend on the scale variables, so the similarity matrix is no longer independent of the transformation being learned. A scale-aware version is not impossible, but it no longer has the same simple LAP structure.


\subsection{Weight Matching}

Weight matching optimizes layerwise permutations by maximizing weight-space similarity:
\[
\arg\min_{\pi} \|\mathrm{vec}(\Theta^A)-\mathrm{vec}(\pi(\Theta^B))\|^2
=
\arg\max_{\pi}\mathrm{vec}(\Theta^A)\cdot\mathrm{vec}(\pi(\Theta^B)).
\]
Written layerwise, this gives the sum-of-bilinear-assignments problem
\[
\arg\max_{\pi=\{P_i\}}
\langle W_1^{(A)},P_1W_1^{(B)}\rangle_F
+\langle W_2^{(A)},P_2W_2^{(B)}P_1^\top\rangle_F
+\cdots+
\langle W_L^{(A)},W_L^{(B)}P_{L-1}^\top\rangle_F.
\]
Similarly to activation matching, adding scale changes the structure of the optimization. If each permutation is augmented with a diagonal scale matrix, adjacent layers become coupled through both scales and inverse scales. The resulting objective is no longer a clean sequence of assignment updates, making a minimal scale-aware extension structurally invasive.


\subsection{STE-Based Methods}

The straight-through estimator (STE) variant of Git Re-Basin optimizes an interpolation-related loss while projecting relaxed variables back to hard permutations:
\[
\min_{\widetilde{\Theta}_B}
\mathcal{L}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}(\Theta^A+\mathrm{proj}(\widetilde{\Theta}_B))\right),
\qquad
\mathrm{proj}(\Theta)
=
\arg\max_{\pi}
\mathrm{vec}(\Theta)\cdot\mathrm{vec}(\pi(\Theta^B)).
\]

Here \(\widetilde{\Theta}_B\) is an unconstrained set of parameters that approximates some permuted version \(\pi(\Theta^B)\), allowing the permutation to be optimized implicitly. The difficulty is that the projection \(\mathrm{proj}(\cdot)\) is non-differentiable. The STE handles this by using the projected, valid permutation in the forward pass when evaluating the loss, but passing gradients backward as if the unconstrained parameters \(\widetilde{\Theta}_B\) had been used directly. This keeps the evaluated model on the permutation-constrained set while still providing usable gradients for optimization.
Incorporating scale would require replacing the permutation action by a mixed discrete-continuous transformation. This is possible in principle, but it combines straight-through discrete optimization with continuous scale variables and inverse transformations across layers.

\subsection{Sinkhorn Re-Basin}

Sinkhorn re-basin replaces hard layerwise permutations with differentiable soft permutations. For each hidden layer \(\ell\), it learns a matrix of logits \(Z_\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{n_\ell \times n_\ell}\), where \(n_\ell\) is the width of the layer. The entries of \(Z_\ell\) are unconstrained real-valued scores: a large value of \((Z_\ell)_{ij}\) indicates that unit \(i\) in one model is currently considered a strong candidate to match unit \(j\) in the other model. Thus, \(Z_\ell\) is not itself a permutation matrix, but a learnable score matrix from which a soft alignment is constructed.

From these logits, the method computes
\[
S_\ell=\mathrm{Sinkhorn}(Z_\ell/\tau),
\]
where \(\tau>0\) is the temperature parameter. Mathematically, given a score matrix \(A\), the Sinkhorn operator first exponentiates the scores and then repeatedly normalizes rows and columns:
\[
K=\exp(A),\qquad
K^{(t+\frac{1}{2})}_{ij}
=
\frac{K^{(t)}_{ij}}{\sum_j K^{(t)}_{ij}},
\qquad
K^{(t+1)}_{ij}
=
\frac{K^{(t+\frac{1}{2})}_{ij}}{\sum_i K^{(t+\frac{1}{2})}_{ij}}.
\]
After several iterations, \(K^{(t)}\) is approximately doubly stochastic and is used as \(S_\ell\). Thus, the Sinkhorn operator converts the unconstrained scores into a nonnegative matrix whose rows and columns each sum to one. This can be interpreted as a relaxed one-to-one matching between hidden units. When \(\tau\) is small, \(S_\ell\) becomes sharper and closer to a hard permutation; when \(\tau\) is larger, the alignment remains more diffuse. This relaxes the discrete permutation search into continuous optimization over the Birkhoff polytope.

The original Sinkhorn re-basin procedure is therefore:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Initialize layerwise alignment logits \(Z_\ell\) and compute soft permutation matrices \(S_\ell=\mathrm{Sinkhorn}(Z_\ell/\tau)\).
    \item Transform one model using these soft alignments: apply \(S_\ell\) to reorder the output coordinates of layer \(\ell\), and apply \(S_{\ell-1}^{\top}\) on the input side of the next layer.
    \item Interpolate between model \(A\) and the transformed version of model \(B\), and evaluate an interpolation-related objective, such as midpoint loss, on real data. During this step, the model is still transformed using the soft matrices \(S_\ell\), not hard permutations.
    \item Backpropagate through the soft alignments and update \(Z_\ell\) by gradient descent.
\end{enumerate}

After optimization, each soft alignment matrix \(S_\ell\) can be projected to a hard permutation matrix \(P_\ell\) by solving a linear assignment problem, typically with the Hungarian algorithm. This hard projection is then used for final evaluation.

For a true permutation matrix \(P\), the matching between adjacent layers is exact because \(P^{-1}=P^\top\). During Sinkhorn training, however, \(S_\ell\) is only a soft matrix, so directly inverting it is not used. Instead, the implementation uses the differentiable approximation described above: it applies \(S_\ell\) on the output side of one layer and \(S_{\ell-1}^{\top}\) on the input side of the next layer. After projection to a hard permutation, this reduces to the exact permutation action.

Its main advantage for the present chapter is that the alignment variables are already optimized in a differentiable way under the actual quantity of interest, namely interpolation loss, rather than only under a surrogate matching score. This makes Sinkhorn the cleanest base method for adding scale: positive scale variables can be optimized jointly with the soft permutation variables in the same gradient-based loop.

\section{Scale-Aware Sinkhorn Re-basin Formulation}
\label{sec:scale_aware_sinkhorn}

The proposed extension is intentionally minimal. For each hidden layer, the soft permutation matrix is augmented with a positive diagonal scale matrix:
\[
S_\ell=\mathrm{Sinkhorn}(Z_\ell/\tau),
\qquad
D_\ell=\mathrm{diag}(e^{u_\ell}),
\qquad
M_\ell=S_\ell D_\ell.
\]
Here \(Z_\ell\) are the Sinkhorn logits, \(u_\ell\in\mathbb{R}^{n_\ell}\) are learnable log-scale parameters, and the exponential ensures positive scaling. The original Sinkhorn transformation \(S_\ell\) is therefore replaced by \(M_\ell=S_\ell D_\ell\).

Using the same layer-to-layer correction rule introduced earlier for permutation symmetry, but now replacing the permutation matrices \(P_\ell\) by monomial matrices \(M_\ell\), we obtain the corresponding hard scale-aware transformation. The new ingredient is that the correction on the input side now involves the inverse monomial matrix rather than only a transpose. If \(M_{\ell-1}=P_{\ell-1}D_{\ell-1}\) is a hard monomial matrix, with \(P_{\ell-1}\) a permutation matrix, then
\[
M_{\ell-1}^{-1}
=
D_{\ell-1}^{-1}P_{\ell-1}^{\top}.
\]
During soft Sinkhorn training, however, \(S_{\ell-1}\) is still only a soft alignment matrix, not a true permutation, so the exact inverse \(M_{\ell-1}^{-1}\) is not used directly. Instead, the same idea as above is used, now with scaling added in. The output coordinates of layer \(\ell\) are transformed by \(S_\ell D_\ell\), while the input coordinates of the next layer are compensated by \(D_{\ell-1}^{-1}S_{\ell-1}^{\top}\). After the soft alignment is projected to a hard permutation, this reduces to the exact scaled permutation transformation above.

Given the transformed model \(B'\), interpolation is evaluated at the midpoint
\[
\theta_{1/2}=\frac{1}{2}\theta^A+\frac{1}{2}\theta^{B'}.
\]
In the experiments reported here, the training objective is the midpoint interpolation loss, together with a small regularizer on the log-scales that biases the solution toward identity scaling and controls the gauge freedom:
\[
J_{\mathrm{bar}}
=
\mathcal{L}(\theta_{1/2}),
\qquad
J_{\mathrm{scale}}
=
\sum_{\ell}\|u_\ell\|_2^2,
\qquad
J=J_{\mathrm{bar}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{scale}}J_{\mathrm{scale}}.
\]
This regularization also discourages overfitting to the calibration subset. Overall, the formulation directly tests whether adding positive scaling to Sinkhorn re-basin reduces the interpolation barrier relative to permutation-only Sinkhorn.

\subsection{Scale Finetuning with Frozen Permutations}

Besides joint optimization of permutation and scale, one may also introduce scaling only after a permutation optimization stage has already been completed. This gives a general post-processing procedure that does not depend on Sinkhorn specifically. Once any permutation-finding method has produced valid hard layerwise permutation matrices \(P_\ell\), those matrices can be kept fixed and the corresponding scale variables can then be optimized separately.

The procedure therefore has two stages. First, some permutation method is used to obtain hard layerwise permutations \(P_\ell\). These may come from Sinkhorn after projection, from weight matching, from activation matching, or from any other alignment method that returns valid permutation matrices. Second, these permutations are frozen and only the scale variables are optimized. At this stage, no Sinkhorn logits \(Z_\ell\) are introduced, no soft permutation matrices are computed, and no Hungarian projection is performed during optimization. The correspondence between hidden units or channels is treated as fixed throughout. The only trainable variables are the log-scale parameters \(u_\ell\), which define positive diagonal matrices
\[
D_\ell=\operatorname{diag}(e^{u_\ell}).
\]

With the permutations fixed, model \(B\) is reparameterized using the same layer-to-layer correction rule as in Section~\ref{sec:scale_aware_sinkhorn}, but now with \(P_\ell\) fixed and only the diagonal scales varying. Equivalently, each hidden layer is transformed by a monomial matrix \(M_\ell=P_\ell D_\ell\), where the permutation part is constant and the diagonal part is optimized. The output side of layer \(\ell\) is scaled by \(D_\ell\), while the input side of the next layer is compensated by \(D_\ell^{-1}\). Because the permutations are fixed, this stage is a purely continuous optimization over the scale parameters.

The optimization objective, midpoint evaluation, and scale regularization are exactly the same as in Section~\ref{sec:scale_aware_sinkhorn}. The only difference is which variables are optimized: during this stage, gradients are taken only with respect to \(u_\ell\). The permutations \(P_\ell\) remain fixed, so the optimization can only improve interpolation by rescaling already-matched hidden units or channels, not by changing their correspondence. This isolates the contribution of scale from the permutation search itself.


\section{XOR Experiments}
The XOR experiments serve as the minimal testbed for separating symmetry from search. In the previous chapter, XOR already showed that permutation-only alignment is limited in narrow networks and improves with width. However, on those experiments alone it is unclear whether adding scale would help because it genuinely captures an additional symmetry, or simply because it makes optimization easier. XOR provides a setting where this distinction can be tested exactly, since exhaustive search over all permutations is feasible.

\subsection{Methodology}
\label{subsec:xor_scale_methodology}

XOR is studied with small ReLU MLPs of architecture \(2\)-\(h\)-\(1\), where \(h \in \{3,5,7\}\). The number of trained networks was not kept fixed across widths: for wider settings fewer networks, and therefore fewer model pairs, were used to keep the total experiment time manageable. All models were trained on the full XOR dataset, and only solutions that achieve \(100\%\) training accuracy and sufficiently low endpoint loss were retained. This yields \(21\) valid model pairs for \(h=3\), \(36\) valid pairs for \(h=5\), and \(10\) valid pairs for \(h=7\). For each pair, linear interpolation in parameter space is evaluated under five alignment modes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{No Alignment}: direct interpolation between the two trained endpoints.
    \item \textbf{Sinkhorn Permutation Only (From Scratch)}: a relaxed permutation is optimized from scratch with Sinkhorn and then projected to a hard permutation.
    \item \textbf{Best Exhaustive Permutation}: all hidden-unit permutations are enumerated and the one with the lowest barrier is selected. This is the oracle best result achievable by permutation alone.
    \item \textbf{Sinkhorn Permutation + Scale (From Scratch)}: permutation and positive hidden-unit scalings are optimized jointly from scratch.
    \item \textbf{Best Exhaustive Permutation + Scale Refinement}: first find the oracle best permutation by exhaustive search, then optimize only the scale variables while keeping that permutation fixed.
\end{enumerate}

The purpose of these modes is to separate two effects. The first is whether a practical relaxed method such as Sinkhorn can find the right correspondence between hidden units. The second, and more important, is whether positive scaling gives a genuine improvement even after the best possible permutation has already been found. If scale refinement improves the exhaustive-permutation baseline, then the benefit cannot be attributed merely to a better search procedure; it must come from the larger symmetry class itself.

For the optimization-based methods, a default hyperparameter configuration is run first. If that default run fails to satisfy the target criterion, a bounded hyperparameter search is triggered. For the scale-based methods, the target is a small barrier threshold (\(\epsilon = 0.01\)). For Sinkhorn permutation-only, the target is the barrier achieved by the best exhaustive permutation for that same pair. The main evaluation metric is the interpolation loss barrier.

\section{Sinkhorn Permutation + Scale on VGGs}

The VGG experiments serve as the large-scale counterpart to the XOR study. On XOR, exhaustive search made it possible to separate the effect of permutation from the effect of scaling exactly. On CIFAR-10 with VGG architectures, exhaustive search is infeasible, so the question becomes whether the same qualitative pattern persists in a realistic high-dimensional setting. In particular, the aim is to test whether adding scale to Sinkhorn-based alignment reduces the interpolation barrier relative to permutation-only alignment, and whether further improvement can be obtained by fixing an already found permutation and optimizing only scale on top of it.

This experiment is a direct continuation of the earlier VGG analysis in Section~\ref{sec:controlled_benchmark}. There, permutation-based alignment improved connectivity but did not achieve full LMC for larger distances between independently trained solutions. The XOR results of the present chapter suggest one possible explanation: permutation may be only part of the relevant symmetry. The VGG experiments therefore test whether the same conclusion extends beyond the minimal synthetic setting.

\subsection{Methodology}

The optimization modes are the same as in the XOR experiment (see Section~\ref{subsec:xor_scale_methodology}). In the VGG setting, however, exhaustive-permutation baselines are infeasible, so we evaluate only the three optimization-based modes. These are applied to fixed endpoint pairs of VGG11, VGG13, VGG16, and VGG19 on CIFAR-10. In all cases, the aligned model is compared to the second endpoint by evaluating test loss along the linear interpolation, and the loss barrier is used as the main metric.

The results do not show uniform dominance of one scale-based variant over the other. For VGG13 and VGG16, optimizing scale after first fixing the permutation yields a lower barrier than joint permutation+scale optimization, whereas for VGG11 and VGG19 the jointly optimized variant performs better. Taken together, the XOR and VGG results suggest a coherent picture. Permutation symmetry captures an important part of the geometry relevant for linear mode connectivity, but it does not appear to be the full story. Positive scaling provides an additional symmetry direction that can systematically reduce interpolation barriers. This does not prove that scaling is sufficient to recover full linear connectivity in realistic architectures, but it does show that, for architectures with homogeneous activation functions, restricting alignment to permutations alone is unnecessarily narrow.

\section{Implications for the One-Basin Modulo Permutation Hypothesis}

The experiments in this chapter also refine the interpretation of the original hypothesis. The \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis} asks whether independently trained networks become linearly connected after accounting for permutation symmetry. The present results suggest that this formulation may be too restrictive. In practice, two networks may fail to exhibit LMC not because they lie in genuinely different basins, but because permutation alone does not account for all function-preserving transformations that matter for interpolation geometry.

A more general view suggested by these results is that linear connectivity should perhaps be studied modulo a broader symmetry group that includes both permutation and scaling. Under this interpretation, the relevant question becomes not whether SGD solutions are linearly connected after permutation, but whether they are linearly connected after choosing appropriate representatives within their full symmetry class.

The present thesis does not resolve that broader question fully. However, it provides evidence for two important claims:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item failure of permutation-only alignment should not automatically be interpreted as evidence against a single-basin view, and
    \item scale symmetry can produce systematic improvements beyond the best permutation alone, both in a controlled minimal setting and in realistic VGG experiments.
\end{enumerate}

These observations strengthen the conclusion of RQ2: when alignment methods fail, this failure may reflect not only optimization limitations, but also an incomplete choice of symmetry class.

\section{Reproducibility Details for Curved-Path VGG16 Experiments}
\label{app:curved_path_repro}

\subsection{Endpoint Training and Endpoint Construction}

For the VGG16 endpoints used in the curved-path experiments, the CIFAR-10 training recipe follows the standard \texttt{pytorch-vgg-cifar10} setup \citep{fu_pytorch_vgg_cifar10}: SGD with momentum \(0.9\), batch size \(128\), initial learning rate \(0.05\), weight decay \(5\times 10^{-4}\), \(200\) training epochs, and the repository's default learning-rate schedule, which halves the learning rate every \(30\) epochs. Training uses the standard CIFAR-10 augmentation pipeline of random horizontal flips and random crops with padding \(4\). Evaluation uses only tensor conversion and normalization. The curved-path experiments reported in the main text use independently trained endpoint seeds \(0\) and \(1\).

The \texttt{seed0--mirror} endpoints are obtained from the trained \texttt{seed0} model by applying a deterministic reverse-order permutation in every hidden layer or hidden channel dimension, with the corresponding compensation in the next layer so that the represented function is unchanged. The \texttt{seed0--randperm} endpoints are obtained from the same trained \texttt{seed0} model by applying a random full-network permutation with permutation seed \(42\). These transformations change parameter coordinates but do not retrain the model.

\subsection{Curve and Polygon Optimization}

All curved-path optimization is carried out with \texttt{dnn-mode-connectivity} \citep{garipov_dnn_mode_connectivity}. For the Bézier runs discussed above, the path family is quadratic Bézier with \(\texttt{num\_bends}=3\), so the two endpoints are fixed and one trainable interior point is optimized. The \texttt{seed0--seed1}, \texttt{seed0--mirror}, and \texttt{seed0--randperm} runs use the same optimization hyperparameters: \(200\) curve-training epochs, learning rate \(0.015\), weight decay \(5\times 10^{-4}\), checkpoint save frequency \(200\), and evaluation on the CIFAR-10 test split (\texttt{use\_test=true}). Train-time augmentation is not disabled in these runs (\texttt{no\_train\_aug=false} where specified), so the standard VGG CIFAR-10 training transform is used during curve optimization. The seed is \(0\) for \texttt{seed0--seed1}, \texttt{seed0--mirror}, and \texttt{seed0--randperm}; for the analogous \texttt{seed1--mirror} run it is \(1\).

For the polygonal-chain experiments used later in the chapter, the same two-endpoint setup is used, but the path family is \texttt{PolyChain} with \(\texttt{num\_bends}=3\). The reported \texttt{seed0--seed1} and \texttt{seed0--mirror} polygon runs use \(150\) training epochs, learning rate \(0.015\), momentum \(0.9\), weight decay \(5\times 10^{-4}\), batch size \(128\), \(4\) data-loading workers, seed \(1\), checkpoint save frequency \(50\), and \texttt{use\_test=true}. In the random-hyperplane experiments, the trainable midpoint is additionally projected after each optimizer step to a random affine subspace through the interpolation midpoint, with codimensions \(k\in\{1,5,10,30\}\). All other polygon-training settings are kept fixed across those codimensions.

\section{Reproducibility Details for Controlled and XOR Experiments}
\label{app:controlled_xor_repro}

\subsection{Controlled LMC Benchmark with Known Permutations}

The controlled LMC benchmark uses the same VGG16/CIFAR-10 training stack as the main VGG experiments, namely \texttt{dnn-mode-connectivity} with the VGG transform, SGD with momentum \(0.9\), batch size \(128\), learning rate \(0.05\), weight decay \(5\times 10^{-4}\), and \(200\) total training epochs. Training uses the standard CIFAR-10 augmentation pipeline during endpoint optimization, so after the split the two branches differ not only in minibatch order but also in the sequence of random data augmentations. Four shared-training splits were prepared: \(0/200\), \(8/192\), \(30/170\), and \(80/120\). In the \(0/200\) case, the shared checkpoint is just the random initialization saved at epoch \(0\) together with the optimizer state so that split training can resume from it. In the other cases, a shared checkpoint is first trained for \(8\), \(30\), or \(80\) epochs with shared seed \(42\), and then split training continues to epoch \(200\) with split seeds \(0\) and \(1\). Checkpoints are saved every \(50\) epochs, and in the shared-training stage the script explicitly sets \texttt{lr\_schedule\_epochs=200} so that the learning-rate schedule is the same as if the model had been trained continuously for the full \(200\) epochs.

The alignment benchmark itself is run on the resulting endpoint pair \((w_0,w_1)\). Both models are loaded as \texttt{VGG16.base(num\_classes=10)} networks. A random full-network VGG16 permutation with permutation seed \(42\) is applied to \(w_1\) to construct \(w_1'\); the permutation is generated layerwise using the project's VGG16 permutation specification, so the transformed checkpoint represents the same function as \(w_1\). Weight matching is then run between \(w_0\) and \(w_1'\) using the VGG16 permutation specification and at most \(100\) iterations. Linear interpolation barriers are evaluated on \(11\) equally spaced interpolation points using cross-entropy loss. These evaluations use CIFAR-10 loaders with batch size \(128\), \(4\) workers, \texttt{use\_test=true}, and evaluation-mode transforms, so no stochastic train-time augmentation is used during barrier measurement. Besides the barrier itself, the script also records the \(\ell_2\) distance and RMS parameter difference between \(w_0\), \(w_1\), \(w_1'\), and the recovered checkpoint.

\subsection{XOR Alignment Experiment}

The minimal XOR alignment experiment is implemented in \texttt{scripts/experiments/xor\_experiment.py}. The dataset is the full four-point XOR set \(\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\}\) with binary labels \(\{0,1,1,0\}\). Each network is a fully connected ReLU MLP with one hidden layer and one scalar output logit. Parameters are initialized uniformly in \([-0.5,0.5]\). Training uses full-batch SGD with learning rate \(0.05\), a maximum of \(5000\) epochs, and a \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} scheduler with factor \(0.5\), patience \(200\), threshold \(10^{-4}\), and minimum learning rate \(5\times 10^{-4}\). Training is stopped once the loss fails to improve by at least \(10^{-4}\) for \(500\) epochs.

For the minimal-width run reported in the thesis, the hidden size is \(2\), and up to \(10\) independently initialized seeds are used, giving \(45\) unordered model pairs. For each pair, the exhaustive alignment search checks both valid hidden-unit permutations: the identity and the swap. The selected permutation is the one that first satisfies \(100\%\) interpolation accuracy and then minimizes interpolation loss. In the width study, the same training routine is reused for hidden sizes \(n\in\{2,3,4,5\}\), but the exhaustive search then ranges over all \(n!\) hidden-unit permutations.

\subsection{XOR Mode-Connectivity Experiment}

The XOR mode-connectivity experiment is implemented in \texttt{scripts/experiments/xor\_curve\_fitting.py}. Endpoint training uses the same XOR dataset and the same SGD-based training procedure described above. The curve-fitting script filters trained endpoints by requiring \(100\%\) training accuracy together with endpoint loss at most \(0.01\). The default script configuration uses \(10\) candidate seeds, hidden size \(3\), and saves outputs under \texttt{results/xor\_unified}; for the thesis experiments, the same code was used with hidden size \(2\) for the minimal-width test and hidden size \(3\) for the next-width comparison.

Two path families are considered: quadratic Bézier curves and polygonal chains. In the default configuration, both use \(\texttt{num\_bends}=3\), which means that the path has a single trainable interior point. Curve fitting uses \(1500\) optimization steps, learning rate \(0.05\), zero weight decay, \(11\) samples of the path parameter \(t\) inside the fitting objective, and \(61\) evaluation points for the final reported path. The default objective is mean loss along the sampled path. When a single trainable point did not suffice, the number of trainable points was increased by using more bends, up to \(10\) trainable points in the reported search.

\textbf{Excluded Experiments}\\
The following experiments were conducted during the research but excluded from the main thesis as they did not directly contribute to testing the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis}. They are documented here for completeness.

\textbf{Initialization Strategy Robustness}

We tested various initialization strategies for Bézier curve training, including biased linear initialization, perturbed midpoint, and sphere-constrained initialization. All strategies converged to low-loss paths with similar barrier heights, indicating that the optimization landscape for path-finding is favorable and the results are not sensitive to initialization choices.

\textbf{Prediction Changes Along Low-Loss Paths}

We tracked how individual datapoint predictions change along trained Bézier curves connecting two modes. While the overall accuracy remains high throughout the path, individual predictions can flip multiple times, suggesting that the path traverses regions with different decision boundaries despite maintaining low loss.

\textbf{Effect of Regularization on Path Properties}

We investigated how different regularization strengths during endpoint training affect the properties of connecting Bézier curves. Stronger regularization produced curves that have less bend and whose points lie closer to the origin.

\textbf{Layer-wise Distance Along Interpolation}

We measured the $\ell_2$ distance between interpolated weights and each endpoint on a per-layer basis along Bézier curves. The analysis revealed that different layers contribute unequally to the overall weight-space distance, with early layers typically showing larger relative changes along the path.
