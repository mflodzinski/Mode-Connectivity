\chapter{Introduction and Problem Statement}
%==============================================================================

\section{Motivation}

Stochastic gradient descent finds trained modes in neural network loss landscapes, 
but the nature of these modes remains poorly understood. Networks trained from 
different initializations converge to different points in parameter space. Do these 
points lie in isolated basins, separated by high-loss barriers? Or do they all 
belong to a single connected region of low loss?

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.73\linewidth]{example_loss_landscapev2.png}
    \caption{Example loss landscape in parameter space: optimization trajectories 
    from different random initializations converge to distinct local minima. The 
    axes $u_1$ and $u_2$ denote directions in parameter space.}
    \label{fig:intro_loss_landscape}
\end{figure}

Multiple isolated basins would mean that initialization is critical and that 
independently trained models occupy genuinely different, separated regions of the 
loss landscape. A single basin, on the other hand, would mean that distinct modes 
are fundamentally related, with their apparent diversity arising partly from 
symmetries in the parameterization.

This question has both theoretical and practical implications. Theoretically, it 
concerns the geometry of neural network loss landscapes: whether independently 
trained solutions are truly separated, or whether their apparent separation is an 
artifact of overparameterized and symmetric representations. Practically, the 
answer determines when operations such as weight-space model averaging can be 
expected to succeed. Averaging neural network weights is only meaningful when the 
models being averaged are compatible under linear interpolation; otherwise, the 
average may cross a high-loss barrier and produce a dysfunctional network.

This thesis therefore studies whether independently trained neural networks can be 
made linearly connected by accounting for permutation symmetries. If this is 
possible, then models that appear separated in the raw parameter space may in fact 
belong to the same underlying basin modulo permutation. If it is not possible, then 
the failures of linear interpolation may reflect genuine structure in the loss 
landscape rather than merely a misalignment of hidden units or channels.

\section{Problem Statement}

\citet{entezari2021role} conjectured that the apparent separation between SGD
minima may largely be an artifact of ignoring permutation symmetries in neural
network parameterizations. They state the conjecture as follows:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Most SGD solutions belong to a set $S$ whose elements can be permuted in
such a way that there is no barrier on the linear interpolation between any two
permuted elements in $S$.}
\end{quote}

The statement does not claim that the original parameter vectors in $S$ are directly linearly connected.
Rather, it claims that each minimum can be replaced by a functionally equivalent
permuted representative, obtained by reordering hidden units or channels, such
that linear interpolation between the permuted representatives has no loss
barrier. In other words, the conjecture suggests that many apparently distinct
SGD minima may lie in the same linearly connected basin once permutation
symmetries are taken into account.

This conjecture suggests that the loss landscape may be fundamentally simpler
than it appears, with apparently distinct basins corresponding to different
permutation representatives of the same underlying region. However, the conjecture
remains unproven, and existing empirical evidence is inconclusive.

Therefore, the central question this thesis addresses is:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Do independently trained neural networks admit permutations under which the linear interpolation between them remains low in loss or, when applicable, classification error?}
\end{quote}

\section{Limitations of Existing Evidence}

Current empirical tests of the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis} face several limitations. 
First, successful demonstrations of linear mode connectivity have been restricted 
to specific architecture–dataset combinations. When alignment methods are applied 
to more challenging architectures such as VGG16 on CIFAR-10, they fail to achieve 
full linear mode connectivity—even when the networks achieve similar, high test 
accuracy and agree on the vast majority of predictions \citep{ainsworth2023git}, indicating highly similar predictive behavior. This raises a fundamental question:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Do alignment methods fail because they are unable to find the correct permutation within a vast combinatorial search space?
    \item Or do multiple basins genuinely exist, meaning that no permutation achieving linear mode connectivity exists for those models?
\end{itemize}

The difficulty of resolving this question theoretically comes from the intrinsic 
complexity of modern neural networks. Their loss landscapes are high-dimensional, 
non-convex, and exhibit symmetries due to parameter permutations and 
scaling invariances. As a result, rigorous global statements about basin structure 
are notoriously challenging to establish.

To conclusively prove or disprove the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis}, 
one would need to either:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Develop a robust and principled permutation-alignment method that consistently achieves
    linear mode connectivity across architectures and datasets, demonstrating that apparent
    barriers arise solely from misalignment in parameter space rather than from genuinely distinct basins, or
    \item Prove that for at least one non-degenerate architecture–dataset configuration, 
    no permutation within the full symmetry group yields linear mode connectivity, 
    thereby establishing the existence of genuinely distinct basins.
\end{itemize}

\section{Research Questions}

This thesis investigates the validity of the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis} through the following research questions:

\begin{enumerate}  
    \item[\textbf{RQ1}] \textbf{(Hypothesis Validity)} Do independently trained neural networks admit permutations under which the linear interpolation between them remains low in loss or, when applicable, classification error?

    \item[\textbf{RQ2}] \textbf{(Method Limitations)} When alignment methods fail to achieve linear mode connectivity, is this due to limitations of the methods or evidence against the hypothesis?

    \item[\textbf{RQ3}] \textbf{(Counterexample Search)} Can one identify a network pair or architecture--dataset setting for which it can be shown that no permutation yields low-barrier linear interpolation?

    \item[\textbf{RQ4}] \textbf{(Beyond Permutation Symmetry)} If permutation symmetry alone is not sufficient to bring one network into the basin of another, do additional symmetries need to be taken into account?
\end{enumerate}
\noindent We assume throughout that networks share the same architecture, core training set, loss function, and hyperparameters---differing only in random initialization, batch ordering, and random augmentations. The specific architectures, datasets, and experimental configurations are detailed in the Appendix.


\section{Contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Nonlinear low-loss paths are widespread:} We show that nonlinear low-loss connections between independently trained models remain easy to find even under random affine constraints, indicating that such paths are abundant in parameter space.
    
    \item \textbf{Controlled benchmark for alignment methods:} We introduce a benchmark with known ground-truth permutations, which provides insight into why alignment methods fail and into the role that distance between modes in parameter space plays in successful alignment.

    \item \textbf{A concrete limitation of permutation-only alignment:} We identify architecture--dataset settings in which permutation-only alignment is guaranteed not to yield low interpolation loss.

    \item \textbf{Scale-augmented alignment:} We extend permutation alignment to include scale symmetry and show that, when applicable, this can improve interpolation barriers beyond permutation-only alignment.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Definitions and Theoretical Foundations}
\label{sec:definitions} 
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Loss Landscapes}

The loss landscape of a neural network is the function $\mathcal{L}: \Theta \to \mathbb{R}$ mapping parameters $\theta \in \Theta$ to loss. Understanding the geometry of this landscape---particularly the structure of minima and the paths connecting them---is fundamental to understanding why neural network optimization succeeds.

\subsection{Basin}

A basin in the loss landscape is informally a connected region of parameter space containing a local minimum, where gradient-based optimization initialized within this region will converge to that minimum. More precisely, for a minimum $\theta^*$, its basin is the set of parameter initializations from which gradient descent converges to $\theta^*$.

For the purposes of this work, we use an analogous connectivity-based definition: two modes $\theta_A$ and $\theta_B$ are considered to lie in the same basin if they can be connected by a linear path along which the loss remains low, i.e., close to the endpoint losses. This definition focuses on the geometry of the loss landscape rather than on the convergence behavior of gradient descent. In particular, if the loss remains approximately constant along a path between two converged models, then the path does not cross a high-loss barrier, suggesting that the two solutions, and every point on that intepolation, belong to the same linearly connected low-loss region.


\subsection{Barrier}

The barrier \citep{entezari2021role} quantifies the difficulty of linearly interpolating between two local minima. Let $\theta_A$ and $\theta_B$ be local minima in parameter space, and let $C(\theta)$ denote a scalar evaluation criterion:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:barrier}
\text{barrier}(\theta_A, \theta_B; C) =
\sup_{\lambda \in [0,1]}
\left[
C\!\left((1-\lambda)\theta_A + \lambda \theta_B\right)
-
\left((1-\lambda)C(\theta_A) + \lambda C(\theta_B)\right)
\right]
\end{equation}

Here $C$ may denote either the loss or the classification error, yielding a loss barrier or an error barrier respectively. In practice, the barrier is approximated by evaluating the criterion at discrete interpolation points (e.g., $\lambda = 0.0, 0.1, \ldots, 1.0$).

\textbf{Interpretation:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Barrier $\approx 0$: Flat or convex interpolation; the two modes are linearly connected.
    \item Barrier $> 0$: A loss/error ``hill'' exists between the two modes.
    \item Barrier $\approx$ loss/error at random initialization: Modes are effectively disconnected.
\end{itemize}

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{imabarrier_example_v3ge.png}
    \caption{Example of interpolation barriers for the classification loss along a linear path between two modes.}    \label{fig:placeholder}
\end{figure}
\subsection{Mode Connectivity}

Two modes are \textbf{mode connected} if there exists a continuous path $\gamma : [0,1] \to \Theta$ with $\gamma(0)=\theta_A$ and $\gamma(1)=\theta_B$ such that:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\gamma(t)) \approx \mathcal{L}(\theta_A) \approx \mathcal{L}(\theta_B) \quad \forall\, t \in [0,1]
\end{equation}

This definition allows curved paths (Bézier curves, polygonal chains), which is less restrictive than linear mode connectivity.


\subsection{Linear Mode Connectivity}

Two networks $\theta_A$ and $\theta_B$ are \textbf{linearly mode connected} (LMC) if the loss along the linear interpolation $\theta_\lambda = (1-\lambda)\theta_A + \lambda\theta_B$ remains low for all $\lambda \in [0,1]$. The literature does not prescribe a universal threshold for ``low.'' In practice, LMC is typically considered to hold when the interpolated model remains functional---e.g., when test accuracy at any point along the interpolation stays within a few percentage points of the endpoint accuracies \citep{ainsworth2023git}, or equivalently, when the loss barrier is negligible compared to the loss at random initialization.

\subsection{Permutation Symmetry}

Neural networks admit many parameterizations that represent exactly the same function. The symmetry most relevant for this thesis is permutation symmetry: hidden units or channels may be reordered, as long as the next layer is adjusted accordingly \citep{hechtnielsen1990algebraic}. The same idea also appears in other architectures, for example in attention-based models where heads can often be permuted without changing the represented function. For the purposes of this thesis, however, it is sufficient to consider fully connected hidden layers and convolutional layers.

\paragraph{Hidden layers.}
For two consecutive fully connected layers,
\[
f(x) = W_2 \,\sigma(W_1 x),
\]
where \(W_1\) is the weight matrix of the first layer, \(W_2\) is the weight matrix of the second layer, and \(\sigma\) denotes the activation function. Let \(P\) be a permutation matrix acting on the hidden units. If the first layer is permuted on its output side and the second layer is compensated on its input side, then the function is unchanged:
\[
\widetilde{f}(x)
=
(W_2 P^\top)\,\sigma(P W_1 x)
=
W_2 P^\top P \,\sigma(W_1 x)
=
W_2 \sigma(W_1 x)
=
f(x).
\]
Thus, reordering hidden neurons and undoing the same reordering in the next layer leaves the network invariant. The same idea extends directly to deeper fully connected networks. If a network has weight matrices \(W_1,\dots,W_L\), and if each hidden layer \(\ell\) is permuted by a permutation matrix \(P_\ell\), then the transformed weights are
\[
W_1' = P_1 W_1,\qquad
W_\ell' = P_\ell W_\ell P_{\ell-1}^{\top}\;(\ell=2,\dots,L-1),\qquad
W_L' = W_L P_{L-1}^{\top}.
\]
Here, left multiplication by \(P_\ell\) permutes the output coordinates of layer \(\ell\), while right multiplication by \(P_{\ell-1}^{\top}\) compensates for the permutation of the previous hidden layer on the input side. The first layer has no preceding hidden layer, so only left multiplication appears, while the output layer has no following hidden layer, so only the right-side compensation remains.

\paragraph{Convolutional layers.}
The same argument holds for convolutional channels. Let the first convolution produce hidden feature maps
\[
h = \sigma(W_1 * x),
\]
and let $P$ permute the output channels of this layer. Then the permuted hidden representation is
\[
\widetilde{h} = \sigma((P W_1) * x) = P h,
\]
and the next convolution can compensate by permuting its input-channel dimension with $P^\top$:
\[
(W_2 P^\top) * \widetilde{h}
=
(W_2 P^\top) * (P h)
=
W_2 * h.
\]
Therefore, permuting the channels of one convolutional layer and applying the inverse permutation to the input channels of the next layer does not change the network function. This extends in the same way to deeper convolutional networks, with exactly the same algebraic form as above. The only difference is how the multiplications are interpreted: for convolutional kernels, the left multiplication acts on the output-channel axis and the right multiplication acts on the input-channel axis. The reason the same formula applies is that a convolution is still linear in its channel coordinates: reordering the output channels of one layer simply reorders the feature maps passed to the next layer, so the next layer can undo this exactly by applying the inverse permutation on its input-channel dimension.

In both cases, permutations can be chosen independently at each hidden layer or hidden channel dimension. This creates a large family of parameter vectors that are functionally identical, even though they may be far apart in Euclidean parameter space.

\subsection{The One-Basin Modulo Permutation Hypothesis}

\citep{entezari2021role} conjectured that most SGD minima belong to a set whose elements can be permuted so that linear interpolation between any two permuted elements has low barrier---the one-basin modulo permtuation hypothesis. Formally, for most pairs of SGD minima $\theta_A, \theta_B$:
\begin{equation}
\exists\, P \in \mathcal{P} \text{ such that } \theta_A \text{ and } P(\theta_B) \text{ are linearly mode connected.}
\end{equation}
This accounts for the fact that $\theta_B$ and $P(\theta_B)$ compute the same function but occupy different locations in parameter space due to permutation symmetry. Importantly, even if $\theta_A$ and $P(\theta_B)$ are LMC, they may still compute different functions---the hypothesis claims only that they lie in the same low-loss basin, not that all SGD minima are functionally equivalent.

\subsection{Hierarchy of Connectivity Claims}

    \centering
    \caption{Hierarchy of connectivity claims, from weakest to strongest.}
    \label{tab:hierarchy}

The \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis} corresponds to ``LMC modulo permutation''---stronger than general mode connectivity but weaker than claiming raw linear connectivity.

\section{Weight Averaging and Model Merging}
\label{sec:weight_averaging}
One practical reason to study linear mode connectivity is that weight averaging 
only works when the models being averaged are compatible in parameter space. Given 
two models $\theta_A$ and $\theta_B$, their average is the midpoint of the linear 
interpolation,
\[
\theta_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{1}{2}(\theta_A + \theta_B).
\]
Thus, if the linear path between two models crosses a high-loss barrier, the 
averaged model may perform poorly even when both endpoints have low loss. This 
makes linear connectivity stronger and more practically relevant for model 
averaging than general mode connectivity: two modes may be connected by a nonlinear 
low-loss path, while their direct linear interpolation still has a high barrier.

Several successful weight-averaging methods operate in settings where such 
compatibility is encouraged by design. Stochastic Weight Averaging (SWA) averages 
checkpoints visited along a single SGD trajectory, typically using a constant or 
cyclical learning-rate schedule. Since these checkpoints come from the same 
training run, they are not fully independent minima; rather, they are nearby 
points sampled from the same region of the loss landscape. Averaging them often 
produces a solution near the centre of a wider, flatter optimum and can improve 
generalization while retaining the inference cost of a single model 
\citep{izmailov2018averaging}.

A related example is given by model soups, where multiple models fine-tuned from 
the same pretrained initialization are averaged in weight space. These models may 
be trained with different hyperparameters, but because they share the same 
pretrained starting point, they are more likely to remain in a common linearly 
connected low-error basin than models trained independently from random 
initialization. \citet{wortsman2022model} show that such averaging can improve 
accuracy and robustness without increasing inference-time or memory cost, unlike a 
conventional ensemble.

These examples show that weight averaging can be beneficial once linear 
compatibility in parameter space is present. However, they avoid the harder case 
of averaging independently trained networks. Models trained from different random 
initializations may be more diverse, but direct averaging of their weights often 
crosses a high-loss barrier and produces a dysfunctional network. If permutation 
symmetries could be used to move independently trained models into the same 
linearly connected basin, then the benefits of weight averaging could potentially 
extend beyond shared-trajectory or shared-initialization settings.

This is the motivation behind permutation-aware model merging methods such as Git 
Re-Basin, which attempt to align independently trained models before averaging 
them in weight space \citep{ainsworth2023git}. \citet{ainsworth2023git} test this 
idea by merging multiple independently trained MLPs on MNIST and report that the 
resulting merged model achieves lower test loss than the individual input models. 
Thus, permutation-aware merging suggests a possible way to obtain some benefits of 
combining independently trained models in a single network, without the memory or 
inference-time overhead of a conventional ensemble, although the merged model may 
still underperform an explicit ensemble in terms of loss and accuracy.

\section{Evidence For and Against the Hypothesis}
\label{sec:literature}
%==============================================================================

This section reviews the evidence for and against the \textit{one-basin modulo 
permutation hypothesis}. It begins with curved-path mode connectivity results, 
which show that many trained networks can be connected by nonlinear low-loss paths. 
It then discusses the statistical evidence of \citet{entezari2021role} and the 
development of explicit permutation-finding methods.

\subsection{Mode Connectivity via Curved Paths}

The study of mode connectivity began with the discovery that trained networks can be connected by low-loss \textit{curved} paths, even when linear interpolation fails.

\citet{draxler2018essentially} used the Nudged Elastic Band (NEB) algorithm from computational chemistry to find minimum-energy paths between networks trained on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100. Their key finding: ``minima are perhaps best seen as points on a single connected manifold of low loss, rather than as the bottoms of distinct valleys.''

\citet{garipov2018loss} showed that simple parametric curves suffice to connect minima. They proposed two approaches:

\textit{Quadratic Bézier curve:}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bezier}
\theta(t) = (1-t)^2 \theta_A + 2t(1-t)\theta_{\text{mid}} + t^2 \theta_B
\end{equation}

\textit{Polygonal chain:}
\begin{equation}
\theta(t) = \begin{cases}
2(t\theta_{\text{mid}} + (0.5 - t)\theta_A) & 0 \leq t \leq 0.5 \\
2((t - 0.5)\theta_B + (1 - t)\theta_{\text{mid}}) & 0.5 \leq t \leq 1
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $\theta_{\text{mid}}$ is a trainable midpoint optimized to minimize the expected loss along the path.

These results establish that many minima are connected by nonlinear low-loss paths, 
suggesting that the loss landscape has a simpler structure than previously thought. 
However, the existence of curved paths does not imply linear mode connectivity. 
Even a single bend point means that the path avoids the direct linear interpolation, 
leaving open whether two modes can be connected by a straight low-loss path.



\subsection{The Entezari Conjecture and Statistical Evidence}

\citep{entezari2021role} proposed the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis} and provided indirect statistical evidence. Their key observation: the distribution of barriers between independently trained networks is statistically indistinguishable from the distribution of barriers between random permutations of the same network.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{enzari.png}
    \caption{Reproduced from \citet{entezari2021role}. 
    \textbf{Left:} Permuting hidden units of networks B, C, D to B', C', D' may eliminate barriers with A while preserving the represented function. 
    \textbf{Middle:} The hypothesis models independently trained networks as random permutations of the same underlying mode. 
    \textbf{Right:} Barrier distributions between independent networks match those between random permutations of the same network across 3000+ networks. Points in the lower-left region mostly correspond to the lowest barrier found after searching over valid permutations using simulated annealing.}
    \label{fig:entezari}
\end{figure}

This evidence is suggestive but indirect. Matching barrier distributions are
consistent with the hypothesis, but they do not by themselves exhibit the
permutations needed to achieve low-barrier interpolation. Conversely, a clear
mismatch between the two distributions would have provided stronger evidence
against the hypothesis. Thus, the central question remained open: could such
permutations actually be found?

\subsection{Explicit Permutation Alignment: Successes and Limitations}

The indirect evidence of \citet{entezari2021role} motivated the development of
methods that explicitly search for permutations. \citet{ainsworth2023git}
addressed this in Git Re-Basin by introducing algorithms that align one model to
another by permuting hidden units or channels before interpolation. Their results
show that permutation alignment can substantially reduce interpolation barriers
across several architecture--dataset combinations. In particular, they report
zero-barrier linear mode connectivity for MLPs on MNIST and for sufficiently wide
ResNet models on CIFAR-10, providing strong empirical support for the one-basin
modulo permutation hypothesis.

However, the results are not uniformly successful. Git Re-Basin also shows that
linear mode connectivity after permutation depends on architecture, width, and
training stage. For example, in their width-scaling experiments on CIFAR-10, the
standard-width VGG16 and ResNet20 models do not fully exhibit linear mode
connectivity after applying permutation. Larger-scale settings such as
ImageNet also remain challenging, with permutation alignment reducing but not
eliminating the interpolation barrier \citep{ainsworth2023git}. These failures
leave open whether the remaining barriers are due to limitations of the
permutation-search methods or because no suitable permutation exists for those
models. Thus, Git Re-Basin provides the first strong explicit evidence that the
conjectured permutations can sometimes be found, but also shows that the
hypothesis is not fully resolved across all architectures and settings.

\section{Mode Connectivity via Curved Paths}

First, we compare low-loss nonlinear connectivity in two settings that are central to the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation} hypothesis. We consider pairs of independently trained models initialized from different random seeds. Second, we consider a model together with a permuted version of itself. This mirrors the comparison made by \citep{entezari2021role} in Figure~\ref{fig:entezari}, who studied barrier distributions for independently trained networks and for networks matched by permutation. Here, instead of focusing only on linear interpolation, we ask whether the same two scenarios also exhibit similarly low barriers along learned nonlinear paths. If they do not, that would suggest that the two cases may not be geometrically equivalent, despite the permutation-based hypothesis.

To test this, we train Bézier curves in two settings: (1) between independently initialized models (`seed0--seed1`), and (2) between a model and a permuted version of itself (`seed0--mirror`). Since low-loss curved paths are already known to exist between independently trained models \citep{garipov2018loss}, one would expect such paths to exist at least as readily between a model and a permuted copy, because the two endpoints implement the same function and differ only by a hidden-unit permutation. 

\subsection{Methodology}

Quadratic Bézier curves are trained with fixed endpoints and a single trainable interior bend, corresponding to the parameterization in equation~\eqref{eq:bezier}. Across the compared settings, the curve-training setup is kept the same so that any difference in the resulting path quality is attributable to the relation between the endpoints rather than to a different optimization schedule. Full hyperparameter details are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:curved_path_repro}.

For the mirror setting, the second endpoint is constructed by reversing the order of hidden units within each hidden layer:
\[
\bigl(P_\ell^{\mathrm{mirror}}\bigr)_{ij} =
\begin{cases}
1, & j = n_\ell + 1 - i, \\
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\]
where $n_\ell$ is the width of hidden layer $\ell$. Thus, unit \(i\) is mapped to position \(n_\ell+1-i\). The same construction applies to convolutional layers, where it reverses the order of output channels. For the random-permutation setting, the second endpoint is obtained instead by applying a full-network random permutation, while keeping the represented function unchanged.

\subsection{Results}


\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{combined_connectivity_te_only.png}
    \caption{Linear and curve-based connectivity for VGG16 on CIFAR10. The main panel shows test error along the full interpolation path. The figure contains three pairwise interpolations/curves between three trained seed models, as well as the mirror-based pairs. The insets show the corresponding curve-only trajectories, that is, the same quantities without linear interpolation, in order to make the differences between seed-seed and seed-mirror pairs more visible.}
    \label{fig:combined_connectivity}
\end{figure}


The main panel of Figure~\ref{fig:combined_connectivity} shows that both settings admit low-loss curved paths, with the error peaking near the midpoint, \(t=0.5\). Measured relative to the linear interpolation between the endpoint errors at the point of maximal path error, the barrier is \(0.87 \pm 0.29\) for different-mode pairs and \(2.38 \pm 0.00\) for mirrored pairs, reported as mean \(\pm\) sample standard deviation. Thus, although the peak path error remains modest in both cases, the mirrored pairs exhibit a substantially larger excess over the endpoint-interpolation baseline. This confirms that curved mode connectivity is present in our setup, since such barrier values still correspond to functional models along the path. At the same time, the insets reveal a systematic difference: the barriers for mirror-permuted pairs are consistently higher than those for independently trained pairs. This is notable because the mirror endpoints are functionally identical, whereas independently trained models need not be. To test whether this effect is specific to the mirror construction or instead reflects a more general property of permutation-related pairs, we next repeat the experiment using random permutations rather than deterministic mirroring.



\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{randpermute_te_only.png}
\caption{Curved mode connectivity between three independently trained networks and between the same networks and their random permutations (without linear interpolation for clarity).}
\label{fig:baseline-bezier-nointerpolation_randpermute}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:baseline-bezier-nointerpolation_randpermute}, random permutations yield low-loss curves that are very similar to those obtained between independently trained models. The error profiles along the optimized Bézier paths still exhibit a visible midpoint peak, but its magnitude remains close to that observed in the independent-seed setting. The barrier for randomly permuted pairs is \(1.07 \pm 0.21\), which is comparable to the previously determined \(0.87 \pm 0.29\) for different-mode pairs. This shows that the elevated barrier observed for mirror-permuted pairs is not a generic consequence of permutation symmetry, but rather a feature of the specific deterministic mirroring construction used to generate those pairs. Why the mirror permutation behaves differently from a random permutation is not clear, but resolving that question is not important for the present thesis. What matters here is that curved low-loss paths remain robust both between independent modes and across random permutation symmetries. Had this not been the case, a clear separation between the random-permutation and independent-seed barriers would have counted as evidence against the hypothesis that these two situations are geometrically equivalent, in the sense illustrated by \citep{entezari2021role} in Figure~\ref{fig:entezari}.



\section{Hyperplane-Constrained Path Finding}
\label{sec:hyper}
The Bézier experiments above show that low-loss curved paths can be found both between independently trained models and between models related by permutation. This raises a more geometric question: how much freedom is actually needed to find such paths? If low-loss paths are rare and occupy only narrow corridors in parameter space, then even a simple geometric constraint should make path optimization much harder. Conversely, if low-loss paths are abundant, then imposing a weak constraint should have little effect. To test this, we constrained the trainable midpoint parameter vector of a three-bend polygonal chain to lie in random affine subspaces that pass through the midpoint of the linear interpolation. Importantly, the codimension constraint is applied only to this trainable point, not to the entire path.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{canvaxd_v2.png}
    \caption{Schematic of path optimization under a hyperplane constraint, adapted from \citet{draxler2018essentially}. The midpoint between two modes is initialized on the linear interpolation and restricted to move inside a fixed hyperplane. A path is then obtained by connecting the endpoints through the optimized midpoint. The constraining hyperplane is not assumed to be the perpendicular bisector of the linear interpolation between the endpoints.}
    \label{fig:hyperplane_constraint_schematic}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Methodology}

For two endpoints $\theta_0$ and $\theta_1$, let
\[
\mathbf{m}=\frac{1}{2}(\theta_0+\theta_1).
\]
The polygonal path is then defined by connecting $\theta_0$ to a trainable midpoint $\theta$ and $\theta$ to $\theta_1$, while requiring only this midpoint $\theta$ to remain inside a randomly chosen subspace through $\mathbf{m}$. The two line segments joining $\theta_0$ to $\theta$ and $\theta$ to $\theta_1$ are not themselves separately constrained. In the implementation, the midpoint is flattened into a single parameter vector, and the constraint is written as
\[
N^\top(\theta-\mathbf{m})=0,
\]
where \(N=[n_1,\dots,n_k]\) contains \(k\) orthonormal random directions in the full parameter space, and \(k\) is the codimension, so the feasible set is the intersection of the \(k\) random hyperplanes
\[
n_i^\top(\theta-\mathbf{m})=0,\qquad i=1,\dots,k.
\]
The random directions are sampled from a standard Gaussian distribution and orthonormalized by Gram--Schmidt. After each gradient step, the trainable midpoint is projected back onto this intersection by removing its components along the constrained directions:
\[
\theta_{\mathrm{proj}}
=
\theta-\sum_{i=1}^k \bigl((\theta-\mathbf{m})^\top n_i\bigr)n_i.
\]
Thus, optimization is free to move inside the \((N-k)\)-dimensional affine subspace orthogonal to the sampled directions, but not along those directions themselves. We varied the codimension \(k\), using \(k\in\{1,5,10,30\}\).



\subsection{Results}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{random_plane_midpoint_codim1_5_10_30_seed0-seed1.png}
    \caption{Comparison of polygonal path optimization when the trainable midpoint is constrained to random subspaces through the interpolation midpoint, for codimensions $1$, $5$, $10$, and $30$.}
    \label{fig:random_planes_comparison}
\end{figure}

The result is that all tested codimensions yield low-loss paths with virtually identical barriers: across this range of codimensions, the differences are negligible. At the same time, the optimized trainable midpoints found by the constrained searches are different, showing that multiple distinct constrained configurations can yield similarly good low-loss connections.

        \toprule
        \textbf{Codimension} & \textbf{Max Test Error} & \textbf{Barrier Height} \\
        \midrule
        1 & 8.19\% & 1.23\% \\

    \caption{Path quality under random hyperplane constraints of different codimensions.}

The interpretation is that constraining a single trainable point by these codimension conditions remains extremely weak relative to the ambient parameter dimension of roughly $15$ million. Even after removing up to $30$ directions from the admissible location of the midpoint, the optimizer still has enough freedom to find essentially the same quality of low-loss connection. The important point is therefore not that a particular constrained subspace is special, but that the low-loss region is broad enough to admit many different constrained connecting points.

This supports a broader picture in which trained modes are not connected only by delicate, low-dimensional corridors. Instead, the low-loss region appears thick enough that even constrained path searches can reach different trainable points while retaining essentially the same path quality.

\section{Controlled LMC Benchmark with Known Permutations}
\label{sec:controlled_benchmark}

In contrast to the Bézier experiments above, which test the existence of curved low-loss paths (mode connectivity), the following experiment focuses on linear mode connectivity (LMC) via straight-line interpolation. LMC is a strictly stronger geometric condition than curved connectivity: failure of LMC does not imply disconnection, but rather linear separation.

The goal of this experiment is to evaluate whether permutation-finding methods succeed when ground truth is available. Prior work has not evaluated alignment methods against known ground-truth permutations. Existing evaluations typically measure only the final barrier after alignment; if the barrier remains high, it is unclear whether (a) the method failed to recover a good permutation that exists, or (b) no such permutation exists, meaning the networks genuinely lie in different basins. Beyond method validation, the shared-split construction used here also provides a controlled family of related endpoint pairs whose barrier can serve as a reference scale when interpreting the barriers of independently trained models.

Importantly, prior work \citep{entezari2021role} focuses on aligning a model with a permuted version of itself---i.e., recovering the exact symmetry that makes two networks functionally identical. In that setting, successful alignment reduces one network to the other up to permutation. Our objective is different: we seek a permutation that aligns two \emph{distinct trained modes} so that they become linearly mode connected, not necessarily identical. The task is therefore not to reconstruct an exact symmetry transformation, but to find a permutation that reduces the LMC barrier between genuinely different modes.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{construction.png}
    \caption{Starting from a shared initialization $\theta_{\mathrm{init}}$, training proceeds jointly until the split point $\theta_{\mathrm{split}}$, after which it diverges to produce two related modes $\theta_0$ and $\theta_1$. A known permutation is then applied to $\theta_1$ to obtain $\theta_1'$, and weight matching attempts to align $\theta_0$ and $\theta_1'$, producing $P(\theta_1')$. The blue and green dotted lines indicate the distances between modes referred to later in the experiment. The axes $u_1$ and $u_2$ denote directions in parameter space.}
    \label{fig:placeholder}
\end{figure}

To create a controlled benchmark, we apply a known permutation $P^*$ to one network in a pair that is already LMC-connected, and test whether the alignment method can recover connectivity. Because a good reference alignment is guaranteed to exist, success validates the method’s ability to recover meaningful alignments. Conversely, failure on this controlled task would indicate that the method is too weak to draw conclusions about the hypothesis from its performance. We adopted the shared initialization with split training approach from \citep{frankle2020linear} to construct these network pairs and weight matching \citep{ainsworth2023git} as a permutation-finding method.

\subsection{Methodology}

We fix \(N\) as the total number of training epochs, chosen to match the number required for this model to converge, and vary \(n\), the number of shared epochs, to control the distance between \(\theta_0\) and \(\theta_1\). Longer shared training (larger \(n\)) produces closer networks, whereas shorter shared training makes them more distant.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Train a model for $n$ epochs with a fixed batch ordering, producing a checkpoint $\theta_{\mathrm{split}}$.
    \item Create two copies of $\theta_{\mathrm{split}}$ and continue training them independently for $N-n$ epochs using different batch orderings. This produces two related endpoints, $\theta_0$ and $\theta_1$.
    \item Apply a known random permutation $P^\ast$ to one endpoint, giving $\theta_1' = P^\ast(\theta_1)$.
    \item Run weight matching between $\theta_0$ and the permuted endpoint $\theta_1'$ to recover alignment $P$.
    \item Test whether the recovered alignment reduces the artificial mismatch by comparing the original $\operatorname{barrier}(\theta_0, \theta_1)$ with the aligned $\operatorname{barrier}\bigl(\theta_0, P(\theta_1')\bigr)$.
\end{enumerate}

In this section, we report \emph{relative} barrier values, i.e.\ the barrier normalized by the average endpoint error. Although the rest of the thesis mainly reports raw loss and error values, the relative barrier is useful in general because it expresses the interpolation deterioration on a scale that is less tied to the absolute endpoint error level of a particular model--dataset setting, making this calibration plot easier to interpret and potentially more comparable across different experimental settings.

To assess robustness, we repeated the experiment over multiple random seeds. For the shared-split pairs, this captures the effect of different training trajectories induced by the batch order and training augmentations after the split, as implemented in \citep{frankle2020linear}. For weight matching, the only source of stochasticity is the order in which layers are aligned. We therefore also estimated the variability of the recovered relative barrier across repeated runs. Full training and evaluation details are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:controlled_benchmark_repro}.


\subsection{Results}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{test_accuracy_barrier.png}
    \caption{
    Relative test error barrier as a function of \(\ell_2\) distance between weight vectors, before and after permutation alignment. Blue and green circles correspond to distances and barriers between \(\theta_0\) and \(\theta_1\), and between \(\theta_0\) and \(P(\theta_1')\), respectively; red squares denote independently trained networks. The labels of the form \(n/(N-n)\) indicate the training split, where the two models are trained jointly for \(n\) epochs and then trained independently for the remaining \(N-n\) epochs.}
    \label{fig:barrier-vs-distance}
\end{figure}

In every case, the recovered barrier value \(\operatorname{barrier}(\theta_0, P(\theta_1'))\) is lower than the original \(\operatorname{barrier}(\theta_0, \theta_1)\), suggesting that weight matching finds permutations that improve connectivity beyond the reference alignment. This shows that weight matching can reliably recover a low-barrier alignment when a good alignment is known to exist.

The important observation is that weight matching is always applied to the pair $(\theta_0,\theta_1')$, where $\theta_1' = P^\ast(\theta_1)$ is obtained by applying a random permutation to $\theta_1$. Because of this artificial permutation, the apparent mismatch between $\theta_0$ and $\theta_1'$ is large in all split settings and is comparable to that of independently initialized models. The role of the $\ell_2$ axis in Figure~\ref{fig:barrier-vs-distance} is to show that the outcome of alignment depends not only on the presence of a removable permutation mismatch, but also on the distance scale of the underlying pair $(\theta_0,\theta_1)$. Later shared-training splits produce pairs that are closer in parameter space, and these are precisely the cases in which weight matching recovers low-barrier alignments. Earlier splits produce more distant pairs, and in those cases the residual barrier after alignment remains substantially larger. Thus, the figure shows that weight matching succeeds most clearly when a good alignment also leaves the models relatively close in parameter space.

This is precisely why the controlled benchmark is useful. For each shared-training split, applying $(P^\ast)^{-1}$ to $\theta_1'$ recovers the original endpoint $\theta_1$, so each run comes with a guaranteed reference alignment, namely the barrier of the constructed pair $(\theta_0,\theta_1)$ before the random permutation was applied. At the same time, weight matching is not constrained to recover exactly this inverse permutation: it is free to search for any permutation of $\theta_1'$ that better aligns it with $\theta_0$. This explains why the recovered barrier can be even lower than the original barrier of $(\theta_0,\theta_1)$. The different runs therefore share the same experimental template, but vary in the intrinsic geometry of the underlying endpoints, controlled by the amount of shared training.

Repeated runs also show that the stochasticity of weight matching is negligible in practice. In our experiments, the estimated standard deviation of the recovered relative barrier is already very small in the hardest case, $0/200$, where it is approximately \(6.81\times 10^{-3}\), and then decreases rapidly, to \(2.93\times 10^{-8}\) for the \(100/100\) split. The same qualitative trend is observed for the original shared-split barriers themselves: earlier splits exhibit slightly larger variance, whereas later splits are essentially deterministic. Thus, the barrier--distance curve in Figure~\ref{fig:barrier-vs-distance} should be understood as a stable empirical pattern rather than a fragile artifact of seed choice.

Moreover, we found that at distance $\approx 28$, corresponding to the $4/196$ split, the models are only weakly LMC-connected, with barriers of around 0.23 relative test error which corresponds to 21.7\% raw test error. This is a particularly informative reference point because its $\ell_2$ distance is comparable to that of the independently initialized pair after weight matching, yet its barrier is lower. The comparison is useful in two ways. First, it shows that even for shared-initialization pairs, linear mode connectivity degrades substantially at large parameter distances. Second, it shows that distance alone is not the whole explanation: even at the same distance scale, the independently trained pair remains more linearly separated than the shared-split pair.

At the same time, the figure also suggests a limitation of distance-based alignment objectives themselves. Weight matching is designed to favor permutations that make the aligned models as similar as possible in parameter space, and it works very well when the underlying pair is already sufficiently close, as in the later shared-training splits. In those cases, when a low-barrier alignment also corresponds to relatively small parameter-space mismatch, weight matching is able to recover it even after a random permutation is applied. For independently trained models, however, it does not recover comparably good alignments. This suggests that if a low-barrier or even LMC-achieving permutation exists in that setting, it is likely not one that brings the aligned models especially close in parameter space. Otherwise, one would expect a distance-oriented method such as weight matching to recover it, just as it does in the later shared-training regimes. A plausible interpretation is therefore that good permutations for independently trained models, if they exist, may reduce the barrier without making the aligned weights particularly close in $\ell_2$. If so, objectives based on weight similarity are not merely imperfect surrogates for linear mode connectivity, but may be fundamentally biased toward the wrong solutions in the most difficult regime.

The shared-split pairs also provide a useful reference curve for interpreting the barriers of independently trained models. In particular, the barrier of the independently trained pair after weight matching is comparable to the barrier of shared-split pairs before applying the random permutation, when the two models were trained jointly for only about \(2\) to \(3\) epochs before diverging. For visual clarity, the \(2/198\) split is not shown separately in Figure~\ref{fig:barrier-vs-distance}, since it largely overlaps with nearby points, but it falls in the same regime and supports the same interpretation. In this sense, the controlled shared-split construction provides an empirical ruler for interpreting the residual barrier of independently trained aligned models.

The analysis above leaves a few possible interpretations. One is that the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis} is too strong: permutation symmetry alone may not be sufficient to make independently trained modes linearly connected, since the alignment method substantially reduces the barrier yet still does not recover the near-zero regime seen for late shared-training splits. Another possibility is that better permutations do exist, but are not favored by objectives that primarily reward weight-space similarity. In either case, the results suggest that the main limitation is not merely the presence of a removable permutation mismatch, but the intrinsic linear separation of the underlying endpoints together with the limitations of current alignment objectives.



\section{XOR Experiment: Validating Alignment on a Minimal Problem}
\label{sec:xor_min}
The experiment above shows that alignment performance depends strongly on the underlying distance between modes. However, it still does not fully resolve whether failures at larger distances are caused by limitations of the alignment algorithm or by the absence of any permutation that would yield LMC. To separate these possibilities, we next study the XOR problem, which provides a minimal and fully analyzable setting for permutation alignment. With a 2-2-1 architecture, there are only two hidden-unit permutations: the identity and a swap. Exhaustive search is therefore trivial, allowing us to verify exactly when alignment succeeds or fails, and why.



Additionally, XOR is interesting because multiple distinct decision boundaries can achieve 100\% classification accuracy. Networks may learn different weight configurations while computing functions that agree on the four training points but differ elsewhere. This allows us to test alignment between networks with both similar and different decision boundaries.

\subsection{Methodology}

We use a minimal-width 2-2-1 MLP with bias terms, ReLU activations, and a single logit output, and train independently initialized networks until they reach perfect accuracy, yielding a collection of endpoint pairs.

For each pair, the two possible hidden-unit permutations are evaluated exhaustively: the identity and the swap. The better permutation is selected by first minimizing the error barrier and then the loss barrier. In this setting, the alignment search is exact. Full training details are given in Appendix~\ref{app:xor_repro}.

These results suggest that for minimal-width networks, the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis} does not hold: networks trained from different initializations can converge to genuinely different basins that no permutation can bridge. Successful alignment occurs only in cases where networks happen to learn nearly identical weights. This motivates testing with wider hidden layers, where overparameterization may allow different initializations to find good permutations.

\subsubsection{Effect of Hidden Layer Width}
\label{subsubsec:effect_hidden_width}

Given the failure of alignment on the minimal 2-2-1 architecture, we investigated whether increasing the hidden layer width enables connectivity. We trained networks with $n \in \{2, 3, 4, 5\}$ hidden neurons and exhaustively searched all $n!$ permutations, selecting the best permutation by prioritizing accuracy (100\% required) and then minimizing loss.

This agrees with \citep{ainsworth2023git}, who empirically observed that wider models exhibit better linear mode connectivity and are easier to align using permutation-finding methods. However, even when accuracy stayed perfect, the midpoint loss was rarely zero: it remained slightly higher than at the trained endpoints, indicating that the interpolated networks classified the XOR points correctly but with lower confidence. \section{XOR Experiment: Validating Mode Connectivity on a Minimal Problem}
\label{sec:xor_mode}
The permutation-alignment experiment above showed that LMC fails after permutation allignment for most pairs in the minimal 2–2–1 XOR architecture. However, failure of linear mode connectivity does not imply the absence of curved connectivity. To determine whether the observed failures reflect genuine structural disconnection or simply the absence of LMC after permutation alignment, we next directly investigate curved mode connectivity in the same minimal setting. We attempted to find low-loss curved paths between independently trained XOR modes using both Bézier curves and polygonal chains.

\subsection{Methodology}

The XOR dataset, model class, and endpoint-training setup are the same as in the previous XOR alignment experiment. The difference is the object being optimized: instead of searching over permutations, we directly optimize curved connecting paths between independently trained endpoints. We evaluate both Bézier curves and polygonal chains, beginning with a single trainable midpoint and then increasing the number of trainable points up to 10 when necessary to test whether additional path flexibility is sufficient to recover a low-loss connection. Full optimization details are summarized in Appendix~\ref{app:xor_repro}.

\subsection{Results}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{unsuccesful_10-14_bezier_3bend.png}
    \caption{No low-loss path found (seeds 10→14): networks with different decision boudaries.}
    \label{fig:placeholder}
\end{figure}

Despite extensive searches, low-loss paths were found only between models that were virtually identical in their weights. For non-trivial pairs, none of the path-finding methods succeeded in discovering a low-loss curved path. This held even for models whose decision boundaries appeared visually identical. These results suggest that, in the minimal-width 2--2--1 architecture, independently trained modes are either genuinely disconnected or connected only by paths that are too difficult to recover with the path parameterizations and optimization procedures used here.

These results mirror our earlier permutation-alignment experiment on the minimal XOR architecture: alignment succeeded only in degenerate cases where the networks were effectively identical up to permutation. The consistent failure to find non-trivial curved connections here suggests that width $m=2$ provides insufficient redundancy for independently trained solutions to lie in an easily recoverable common low-loss manifold.

\subsubsection{Effect of Increasing Width}

To determine at what width the set of trained modes becomes connected, we increased the hidden layer to $m=3$ neurons (2--3--1 architecture). With only one additional hidden unit, all independently trained models were successfully connected by low-loss curved paths, regardless of differences in their internal weight configurations or decision boundaries.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{3hidden_xor_bezier.png}
    \caption{Example of successful low-loss path found on a 2-3-1 architecture between models with different decision boundaries (seeds 3→11).}
    \label{fig:xor-3hidden}

Interestingly, for one seed pair (1→14), none of the path-finding methods succeeded directly, even after increasing the number of trainable points. However, we were able to find low-loss paths between 1→7 and 7→14. Since connectivity is transitive under concatenation, this implies that a low-loss path must exist between 1→14 as well. Indeed, a piecewise construction (a polygonal chain passing through the intermediate mode with 3 trainable points) would connect them by concatenating the two low-loss segments.

The failure of direct optimization in this case indicates that low-loss paths in the $m=3$ setting, while existent, may get stuck in local minima and therefore be difficult to find with a limited path parameterization. This highlights that low-loss path-finding algorithms are imperfect: failure to discover a low-loss path does not necessarily imply its non-existence, particularly in narrow networks with limited representational redundancy.



Overall, these results demonstrate a sharp transition in connectivity behavior between width $m=2$ and $m=3$. Minimal-width XOR modes appear genuinely disconnected in parameter space (except for trivial cases), whereas adding a single hidden neuron provides enough redundancy for the modes to become connected by curved low-loss paths.

\chapter{Beyond Permutation: Scale-Aware Alignment for Linear Mode Connectivity}
\label{chap:scale_aware_alignment}
\label{chap:symmetry-aware}
%==============================================================================

The previous chapters showed that low-loss connectivity and linear mode connectivity are different problems. Curved paths were often easy to find, even when the path search was geometrically constrained, suggesting that trained modes can belong to a broad connected low-loss region. Linear mode connectivity is stricter: it asks whether two endpoints can be connected by the straight line between their parameters. The controlled permutation benchmark showed that permutation alignment works when a good permutation is guaranteed to exist, but its performance degrades as the underlying modes move farther apart. The XOR experiments sharpened this point further: because all permutations could be checked exhaustively, we saw that even the best possible permutation does not always remove the interpolation barrier. This suggests that permutation symmetry may not capture all structure relevant for low-barrier interpolation. 

Another possibility is that permutation invariance does not capture the full symmetry structure relevant for low-barrier interpolation. Neural networks with positively homogeneous activations such as ReLU possess an additional layerwise scaling symmetry. A positively homogeneous activation satisfies \(\sigma(cz)=c\,\sigma(z)\) for all \(c>0\). Therefore, for a two-layer block
\[
f(x)=W_2\,\sigma(W_1x),
\]
one may rescale a hidden unit by a positive constant \(c\) without changing the represented function:
\[
(W_2D^{-1})\,\sigma(DW_1x)
=(W_2D^{-1})D\,\sigma(W_1x)
=W_2\,\sigma(W_1x), \qquad
D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\dots,d_m),\quad d_i>0.
\]
Thus, hidden units can be rescaled by positive constants provided the adjacent layer compensates appropriately, leaving the network function unchanged. Two networks may therefore represent similar computations while differing not only in the ordering of hidden units, but also in the internal scale assigned to those units. This motivates the main question of this chapter:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Can linear mode connectivity be improved by aligning networks modulo a larger symmetry class that includes both permutation and positive scaling?}
\end{quote}

The XOR results make this question particularly concrete. Exhaustive permutation search gave an oracle permutation baseline: for each pair, we knew the best barrier achievable by permutation alone. Whenever a nonzero barrier remained after that oracle alignment, the cause could not be a failure of permutation search. A natural next hypothesis is that part of the remaining barrier comes from scale mismatch between corresponding hidden units.  The present chapter investigates this possibility in two steps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item We first introduce a scale-aware extension of permutation alignment, in which positive scaling is added to the permutation-finding procedure.
    \item We then test this extension on both minimal XOR networks, where exhaustive search provides a permutation-only oracle baseline, and larger networks, where scale-aware alignment is evaluated as a practical re-basing method.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Choosing a Base Method for Scale-Aware Alignment}

Before introducing a scale-aware alignment method, we first compare the main re-basin methods to determine which one is best suited for jointly incorporating scale-invariance optimization into permutation finding. The goal is not to show that scaling is impossible to add to other methods, but to identify which framework admits the cleanest minimal modification. The key criterion is whether scale variables can be incorporated without substantially changing the method's original optimization structure.

\subsection{Activation Matching}
Activation matching, introduced in Git Re-Basin \citep{ainsworth2023git}, is data-dependent: the activation matrices are obtained by running a set of inputs through both networks and recording the hidden-layer activations. It then solves a linear assignment problem using these activations. For activations \(Z^{(A)}, Z^{(B)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}\), the layer permutation is chosen as
\[
P_\ell
=
\arg\min_{P \in S_d}
\sum_{i=1}^n \|Z^{(A)}_{:,i} - P Z^{(B)}_{:,i}\|^2
=
\arg\max_{P \in S_d}
\langle P, Z^{(A)}(Z^{(B)})^\top \rangle_F.
\]
Here \(\langle A,B\rangle_F=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ij}\) denotes the Frobenius inner product. Since the objective is linear in the entries of the permutation matrix \(P\), this optimization is a standard linear assignment problem (LAP), for which efficient solvers are available.
This works cleanly when the activation similarity matrix is fixed before solving the assignment problem. Adding scale makes the activations themselves depend on the scale variables, so the similarity matrix is no longer independent of the transformation being learned. A scale-aware version is not impossible, but it no longer has the same simple LAP structure.


\subsection{Weight Matching}

Weight matching \citep{ainsworth2023git}, optimizes layerwise permutations by maximizing weight-space similarity:
\[
\arg\min_{\pi} \|\mathrm{vec}(\Theta^A)-\mathrm{vec}(\pi(\Theta^B))\|^2
=
\arg\max_{\pi}\mathrm{vec}(\Theta^A)\cdot\mathrm{vec}(\pi(\Theta^B)).
\]
Written layerwise, this gives the sum-of-bilinear-assignments problem
\[
\arg\max_{\pi=\{P_i\}}
\langle W_1^{(A)},P_1W_1^{(B)}\rangle_F
+\langle W_2^{(A)},P_2W_2^{(B)}P_1^\top\rangle_F
+\cdots+
\langle W_L^{(A)},W_L^{(B)}P_{L-1}^\top\rangle_F.
\]
Similarly to activation matching, adding scale changes the structure of the optimization. If each permutation is augmented with a diagonal scale matrix, adjacent layers become coupled through both scales and inverse scales. The resulting objective is no longer a clean sequence of assignment updates, making a minimal scale-aware extension structurally invasive.


\subsection{STE-Based Methods}

The straight-through estimator (STE) \citep{ainsworth2023git} optimizes an interpolation-related loss while projecting relaxed variables back to hard permutations:
\[
\min_{\widetilde{\Theta}_B}
\mathcal{L}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}(\Theta^A+\mathrm{proj}(\widetilde{\Theta}_B))\right),
\qquad
\mathrm{proj}(\Theta)
=
\arg\max_{\pi}
\mathrm{vec}(\Theta)\cdot\mathrm{vec}(\pi(\Theta^B)).
\]

Here \(\widetilde{\Theta}_B\) is an unconstrained set of parameters that approximates some permuted version \(\pi(\Theta^B)\), allowing the permutation to be optimized implicitly. The difficulty is that the projection \(\mathrm{proj}(\cdot)\) is non-differentiable. The STE handles this by using the projected, valid permutation in the forward pass when evaluating the loss, but passing gradients backward as if the unconstrained parameters \(\widetilde{\Theta}_B\) had been used directly. This keeps the evaluated model on the permutation-constrained set while still providing usable gradients for optimization.
Incorporating scale would require replacing the permutation action by a mixed discrete-continuous transformation. This is possible in principle, but it combines straight-through discrete optimization with continuous scale variables and inverse transformations across layers.

\subsection{Sinkhorn Re-Basin}

Sinkhorn re-basin \citep{guerrero2023rebasin} replaces hard layerwise permutations with differentiable soft permutations. For each hidden layer \(\ell\), it learns a matrix of logits \(Z_\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{n_\ell \times n_\ell}\), where \(n_\ell\) is the width of the layer. The entries of \(Z_\ell\) are unconstrained real-valued scores: a large value of \((Z_\ell)_{ij}\) indicates that unit \(i\) in one model is currently considered a strong candidate to match unit \(j\) in the other model. Thus, \(Z_\ell\) is not itself a permutation matrix, but a learnable score matrix from which a soft alignment is constructed.

From these logits, the method computes
\[
S_\ell=\mathrm{Sinkhorn}(Z_\ell/\tau),
\]
where \(\tau>0\) is the temperature parameter. Mathematically, given a score matrix \(A\), the Sinkhorn operator first exponentiates the scores and then repeatedly normalizes rows and columns:
\[
K=\exp(A),\qquad
K^{(t+\frac{1}{2})}_{ij}
=
\frac{K^{(t)}_{ij}}{\sum_j K^{(t)}_{ij}},
\qquad
K^{(t+1)}_{ij}
=
\frac{K^{(t+\frac{1}{2})}_{ij}}{\sum_i K^{(t+\frac{1}{2})}_{ij}}.
\]
After several iterations, \(K^{(t)}\) is approximately doubly stochastic and is used as \(S_\ell\). Thus, the Sinkhorn operator converts the unconstrained scores into a nonnegative matrix whose rows and columns each sum to one. This can be interpreted as a relaxed one-to-one matching between hidden units. When \(\tau\) is small, \(S_\ell\) becomes sharper and closer to a hard permutation; when \(\tau\) is larger, the alignment remains more diffuse. This relaxes the discrete permutation search into continuous optimization over the Birkhoff polytope.

The original Sinkhorn re-basin procedure is therefore:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Initialize layerwise alignment logits \(Z_\ell\) and compute soft permutation matrices \(S_\ell=\mathrm{Sinkhorn}(Z_\ell/\tau)\).
    \item Transform one model using these soft alignments: apply \(S_\ell\) to reorder the output coordinates of layer \(\ell\), and apply \(S_{\ell-1}^{\top}\) on the input side of the next layer.
    \item Interpolate between model \(A\) and the transformed version of model \(B\), and evaluate an interpolation-related objective, such as midpoint loss, on real data. During this step, the model is still transformed using the soft matrices \(S_\ell\), not hard permutations.
    \item Backpropagate through the soft alignments and update \(Z_\ell\) by gradient descent.
\end{enumerate}

After optimization, each soft alignment matrix \(S_\ell\) can be projected to a hard permutation matrix \(P_\ell\) by solving a linear assignment problem, typically with the Hungarian algorithm. This hard projection is then used for final evaluation.

For a true permutation matrix \(P\), the matching between adjacent layers is exact because \(P^{-1}=P^\top\). During Sinkhorn training, however, \(S_\ell\) is only a soft matrix, so directly inverting it is not used. Instead, the implementation uses the differentiable approximation described above: it applies \(S_\ell\) on the output side of one layer and \(S_{\ell-1}^{\top}\) on the input side of the next layer. After projection to a hard permutation, this reduces to the exact permutation action.

Its main advantage for the present chapter is that the alignment variables are already optimized in a differentiable way under the actual quantity of interest, namely interpolation loss, rather than only under a surrogate matching score. This makes Sinkhorn the cleanest base method for adding scale: positive scale variables can be optimized jointly with the soft permutation variables in the same gradient-based loop.

\section{Scale-Aware Sinkhorn Re-basin Formulation}
\label{sec:scale_aware_sinkhorn}

The proposed extension is intentionally minimal. For each hidden layer, the soft permutation matrix is augmented with a positive diagonal scale matrix:
\[
S_\ell=\mathrm{Sinkhorn}(Z_\ell/\tau),
\qquad
D_\ell=\mathrm{diag}(e^{u_\ell}),
\qquad
M_\ell=S_\ell D_\ell.
\]
Here \(Z_\ell\) are the Sinkhorn logits, \(u_\ell\in\mathbb{R}^{n_\ell}\) are learnable log-scale parameters, and the exponential ensures positive scaling. The original Sinkhorn transformation \(S_\ell\) is therefore replaced by \(M_\ell=S_\ell D_\ell\).

Using the same layer-to-layer correction rule introduced earlier for permutation symmetry, but now replacing the permutation matrices \(P_\ell\) by monomial matrices \(M_\ell\), we obtain the corresponding hard scale-aware transformation. The new ingredient is that the correction on the input side now involves the inverse monomial matrix rather than only a transpose. If \(M_{\ell-1}=P_{\ell-1}D_{\ell-1}\) is a hard monomial matrix, with \(P_{\ell-1}\) a permutation matrix, then
\[
M_{\ell-1}^{-1}
=
D_{\ell-1}^{-1}P_{\ell-1}^{\top}.
\]
During soft Sinkhorn training, however, \(S_{\ell-1}\) is still only a soft alignment matrix, not a true permutation, so the exact inverse \(M_{\ell-1}^{-1}\) is not used directly. Instead, the same idea as above is used, now with scaling added in. The output coordinates of layer \(\ell\) are transformed by \(S_\ell D_\ell\), while the input coordinates of the next layer are compensated by \(D_{\ell-1}^{-1}S_{\ell-1}^{\top}\). After the soft alignment is projected to a hard permutation, this reduces to the exact scaled permutation transformation above.

Given the transformed model \(B'\), interpolation is evaluated at the midpoint
\[
\theta_{1/2}=\frac{1}{2}\theta^A+\frac{1}{2}\theta^{B'}.
\]
In the experiments reported here, the training objective is the midpoint interpolation loss, together with a small regularizer on the log-scales that biases the optimization toward identity scaling and controls the gauge freedom:
\[
J_{\mathrm{bar}}
=
\mathcal{L}(\theta_{1/2}),
\qquad
J_{\mathrm{scale}}
=
\sum_{\ell}\|u_\ell\|_2^2,
\qquad
J=J_{\mathrm{bar}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{scale}}J_{\mathrm{scale}}.
\]
This regularization also discourages overfitting to the calibration subset. Overall, the formulation directly tests whether adding positive scaling to Sinkhorn re-basin reduces the interpolation barrier relative to permutation-only Sinkhorn.

\subsection{Scale Finetuning with Frozen Permutations}

Besides joint optimization of permutation and scale, one may also introduce scaling only after a permutation optimization stage has already been completed. This gives a general post-processing procedure that does not depend on Sinkhorn specifically. Once any permutation-finding method has produced valid hard layerwise permutation matrices \(P_\ell\), those matrices can be kept fixed and the corresponding scale variables can then be optimized separately.

The procedure therefore has two stages. First, some permutation method is used to obtain hard layerwise permutations \(P_\ell\). These may come from Sinkhorn after projection, from weight matching, from activation matching, or from any other alignment method that returns valid permutation matrices. Second, these permutations are frozen and only the scale variables are optimized. At this stage, no Sinkhorn logits \(Z_\ell\) are introduced, no soft permutation matrices are computed, and no Hungarian projection is performed during optimization. The correspondence between hidden units or channels is treated as fixed throughout. The only trainable variables are the log-scale parameters \(u_\ell\), which define positive diagonal matrices
\[
D_\ell=\operatorname{diag}(e^{u_\ell}).
\]

With the permutations fixed, model \(B\) is reparameterized using the same layer-to-layer correction rule as in Section~\ref{sec:scale_aware_sinkhorn}, but now with \(P_\ell\) fixed and only the diagonal scales varying. Equivalently, each hidden layer is transformed by a monomial matrix \(M_\ell=P_\ell D_\ell\), where the permutation part is constant and the diagonal part is optimized. The output side of layer \(\ell\) is scaled by \(D_\ell\), while the input side of the next layer is compensated by \(D_\ell^{-1}\). Because the permutations are fixed, this stage is a purely continuous optimization over the scale parameters.

The optimization objective, midpoint evaluation, and scale regularization are exactly the same as in Section~\ref{sec:scale_aware_sinkhorn}. The only difference is which variables are optimized: during this stage, gradients are taken only with respect to \(u_\ell\). The permutations \(P_\ell\) remain fixed, so the optimization can only improve interpolation by rescaling already-matched hidden units or channels, not by changing their correspondence. This isolates the contribution of scale from the permutation search itself.


\section{XOR Experiments}
The XOR experiments serve as the minimal testbed for separating symmetry from search. In the previous chapter, XOR already showed that permutation-only alignment is limited in narrow networks and improves with width. However, on those experiments alone it is unclear whether adding scale would help because it genuinely captures an additional symmetry, or simply because it makes optimization easier. XOR provides a setting where this distinction can be tested exactly, since exhaustive search over all permutations is feasible.

\subsection{Methodology}
\label{subsec:xor_scale_methodology}

XOR is studied with small ReLU MLPs of architecture \(2\)-\(h\)-\(1\), where \(h \in \{3,5,7\}\). The number of trained networks was not kept fixed across widths: for wider settings fewer networks, and therefore fewer model pairs, were used to keep the total experiment time manageable. All models were trained on the full XOR dataset, and only modes that achieve \(100\%\) training accuracy and sufficiently low endpoint loss were retained. This yields \(21\) valid model pairs for \(h=3\), \(36\) valid pairs for \(h=5\), and \(10\) valid pairs for \(h=7\). For each pair, linear interpolation in parameter space is evaluated under five alignment modes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{No Alignment}: direct interpolation between the two trained endpoints.
    \item \textbf{Sinkhorn Permutation Only (From Scratch)}: a relaxed permutation is optimized from scratch with Sinkhorn and then projected to a hard permutation.
    \item \textbf{Best Exhaustive Permutation}: all hidden-unit permutations are enumerated and the one with the lowest barrier is selected. This is the oracle best result achievable by permutation alone.
    \item \textbf{Sinkhorn Permutation + Scale (From Scratch)}: permutation and positive hidden-unit scalings are optimized jointly from scratch.
    \item \textbf{Best Exhaustive Permutation + Scale Refinement}: first find the oracle best permutation by exhaustive search, then optimize only the scale variables while keeping that permutation fixed.
\end{enumerate}

The purpose of these modes is to separate two effects. The first is whether a practical relaxed method such as Sinkhorn can find the right correspondence between hidden units. The second, and more important, is whether positive scaling gives a genuine improvement even after the best possible permutation has already been found. If scale refinement improves the exhaustive-permutation baseline, then the benefit cannot be attributed merely to a better search procedure; it must come from the larger symmetry class itself.

For the optimization-based methods, a default hyperparameter configuration is run first. If that default run fails to satisfy the target criterion, a bounded hyperparameter search is triggered. For the scale-based methods, the target is a small barrier threshold (\(\epsilon = 0.01\)). For Sinkhorn permutation-only, the target is the barrier achieved by the best exhaustive permutation for that same pair. The main evaluation metric is the interpolation loss barrier.

\section{Sinkhorn Permutation + Scale on VGGs}

The VGG experiments serve as the large-scale counterpart to the XOR study. On XOR, exhaustive search made it possible to separate the effect of permutation from the effect of scaling exactly. On CIFAR-10 with VGG architectures, exhaustive search is infeasible, so the question becomes whether the same qualitative pattern persists in a realistic high-dimensional setting. In particular, the aim is to test whether adding scale to Sinkhorn-based alignment reduces the interpolation barrier relative to permutation-only alignment, and whether further improvement can be obtained by fixing an already found permutation and optimizing only scale on top of it.

This experiment is a direct continuation of the earlier VGG analysis in Section~\ref{sec:controlled_benchmark}. There, permutation-based alignment improved connectivity but did not achieve full LMC for larger distances between independently trained modes. The XOR results of the present chapter suggest one possible explanation: permutation may be only part of the relevant symmetry. The VGG experiments therefore test whether the same conclusion extends beyond the minimal synthetic setting.

\subsection{Methodology}

The optimization modes are the same as in the XOR experiment (see Section~\ref{subsec:xor_scale_methodology}). In the VGG setting, however, exhaustive-permutation baselines are infeasible, so we evaluate only the three optimization-based modes. These are applied to fixed endpoint pairs of VGG11, VGG13, VGG16, and VGG19 on CIFAR-10. In all cases, the aligned model is compared to the second endpoint by evaluating test loss along the linear interpolation, and the loss barrier is used as the main metric.

\chapter{Conclusion and Discussion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

This thesis investigated whether the apparent separation between independently trained neural networks reflects genuinely distinct basins, or whether it is an artifact of symmetry and parameterization. The results point to a consistent picture: neural network solutions often belong to broad connected low-loss regions, but strict linear compatibility is much harder to obtain. In other words, the landscape appears considerably more connected than raw linear interpolation suggests, yet also less simple than the strongest form of the \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis} would imply.

A central theme running through the thesis is the distinction between \emph{connectivity} and \emph{linear connectivity}. The curved-path experiments \ref{sec:hyper} showed that low-loss paths are often easy to find, even when the path search is geometrically constrained. This strongly suggests that many trained solutions are not isolated in any broad geometric sense. At the same time, the linear interpolation experiments showed that this broad connectedness does not translate automatically into low-barrier straight lines. The main geometric lesson is therefore that curved low-loss connectedness and linear compatibility are fundamentally different properties, and that much of the difficulty lies specifically in understanding the latter.

Viewed from this perspective, permutation alignment explains an important part of the problem, but not all of it. The controlled benchmark \ref{sec:controlled_benchmark} experiment showed that permutation-finding methods are capable of recovering useful alignments when a good reference alignment is known to exist. At the same time, the same benchmark showed that alignment quality degrades in a highly systematic way as the underlying endpoints move farther apart. This makes the failure of linear mode connectivity much more informative: it is not merely that current methods fail randomly, but that the difficulty grows with the geometry of the pair being aligned. The benchmark also suggests that barriers should not be interpreted in isolation. The shared-split construction provides an empirical ruler, showing that an independently trained aligned pair can remain comparable to trajectories that diverged very early in training. Thus, the residual barrier after alignment is not just a number; it describes how close, in interpolation geometry, the aligned pair is to a controlled family of related solutions.

This also clarifies a deeper limitation of existing alignment methods. Methods such as weight matching are ultimately guided by weight-space similarity. The experiments suggest that this works well when a good low-barrier alignment also makes the models relatively close in parameter space, but becomes unreliable in the difficult regime of independently trained solutions. A natural interpretation is that, if better low-barrier representatives exist in such cases, they may not be the ones preferred by objectives based on Euclidean closeness. In that sense, the issue is not only the search procedure, but also the objective being optimized. The thesis therefore suggests that part of the challenge in recovering linear mode connectivity lies in a mismatch between the geometric property of interest and the proxy objectives used by current alignment methods.

The XOR experiments \ref{sec:xor_min} make this point especially sharp. In the minimal setting, exhaustive permutation search is feasible, so the ambiguity between ``the method failed'' and ``no such permutation exists'' can be removed. There, permutation-only alignment is insufficient in genuinely nontrivial cases. This does not by itself settle the large-scale question, but it provides a clean counterexample to the strongest interpretation of the permutation-only hypothesis. At the same time, the width dependence of the XOR results points in the opposite direction: once the model becomes even slightly more overparameterized, both alignment and connectivity become much easier to recover. This suggests that the validity of permutation-based explanations is not a binary matter, but depends strongly on redundancy and width.

The most important extension explored in this thesis is therefore the move beyond permutation symmetry alone. The scale-aware results indicate that, for networks with homogeneous activation functions, permutation is not the only symmetry that matters for interpolation geometry. Positive scaling can systematically improve barriers beyond what permutation alone achieves, including beyond the best permutation baseline in the minimal XOR setting. This makes the broader interpretation of the thesis quite clear: when permutation-only alignment fails, that should not automatically be read as evidence of genuinely distinct basins. It may instead indicate that the chosen symmetry class is too narrow.

\section{Implications for the One-Basin Modulo Permutation Hypothesis}

Taken together, the results suggest that the original \textit{one-basin modulo permutation hypothesis} captures an important intuition, but in a form that is likely too restrictive. The experiments support the idea that many SGD solutions are related and often belong to broad common low-loss regions. However, they also suggest that permutation alone does not reliably account for the remaining linear barriers, and that the presence of a barrier after permutation alignment does not by itself imply truly distinct basins.

A more plausible interpretation is that the relevant question is not whether minima are linearly connected after permutation, but whether they can be made linearly compatible after choosing suitable representatives within a broader symmetry class. Under this view, the present thesis does not refute the broader one-basin intuition; rather, it suggests that its permutation-only formulation may be too narrow. The scale-aware results are especially important in this respect, because they show that systematic improvements are possible once the symmetry class is enlarged.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}

The main limitation of the thesis is that its strongest conclusions come from controlled and minimal settings, whereas the broader architectural conclusions remain empirical. The large-scale experiments focused mainly on VGG-style convolutional networks, and the alignment methods considered, while representative, are not exhaustive. Moreover, some of the optimization-based procedures are sensitive to hyperparameters, which means that the results should be interpreted as evidence about the tested methods rather than a final characterization of all symmetry-aware alignment strategies.

These limitations suggest several natural directions for future work. The most immediate is to study broader symmetry classes beyond permutation and positive scaling, including transformations that preserve performance approximately rather than exactly. A second is to extend the experiments to additional architectures and datasets, especially settings where permutation alignment has historically struggled. A third direction is to develop alignment objectives that use better proxies for linear mode connectivity than weight similarity, without depending entirely on direct interpolation-loss optimization. The former may be geometrically misaligned with the true goal, while the latter is data-dependent and sensitive to optimization details. Designing better objectives will likely require a deeper understanding of which structural properties of the loss landscape determine whether two modes can be made linearly compatible.

\section{Final Remarks}

The overall picture that emerges from this thesis is neither that SGD minima are simply isolated, nor that they all collapse into a single linearly connected basin once neurons are permuted correctly. Instead, trained solutions appear to occupy broad low-loss regions whose internal geometry is shaped by symmetry, width, and the distinction between curved connectedness and strict linear compatibility. Permutation symmetry explains part of this structure, but not all of it. Positive scaling improves the picture further, suggesting that the right geometric description of neural network solutions may require moving beyond permutation-only reasoning.

In that sense, the main contribution of this thesis is not a final answer to the basin question, but a refinement of how that question should be asked. The evidence suggests that the real issue is not simply whether minima are connected, but which notion of connectivity is relevant, which symmetries must be taken into account, and which alignment objectives are actually capable of finding the appropriate representatives. Understanding this more fully remains an open problem, but the experiments here suggest that the answer is likely to involve a richer and more structured view of neural network loss landscapes than either complete disconnection or a simple permutation-only one-basin picture.

\subsection{Endpoint Training and Endpoint Construction}

For the VGG16 endpoints used in the curved-path experiments, the CIFAR-10 training recipe follows the standard \texttt{pytorch-vgg-cifar10} setup \citep{fu_pytorch_vgg_cifar10}. The main settings are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Architecture and dataset:} VGG16 on CIFAR-10.
    \item \textbf{Optimizer:} SGD with momentum \(0.9\).
    \item \textbf{Batch size:} \(128\).
    \item \textbf{Initial learning rate:} \(0.05\).
    \item \textbf{Weight decay:} \(5\times 10^{-4}\).
    \item \textbf{Training length:} \(200\) epochs.
    \item \textbf{Learning-rate schedule:} halve the learning rate every \(30\) epochs.
    \item \textbf{Evaluation split:} CIFAR-10 test split (\texttt{use\_test=true})
    \item \textbf{Training augmentation:} random horizontal flips and random crops with padding \(4\).
    \item \textbf{Endpoint seeds used in the main curved-path experiments:} \(0\) and \(1\).
\end{itemize}

The \texttt{seed0--mirror} endpoints are obtained from the trained \texttt{seed0} model by applying a deterministic reverse-order permutation in every hidden layer or hidden channel dimension, with the corresponding compensation in the next layer so that the represented function is unchanged. The \texttt{seed0--randperm} endpoints are obtained from the same trained \texttt{seed0} model by applying a random full-network permutation with permutation seed \(42\). These transformations change parameter coordinates but do not retrain the model.

\subsection{Curve and Polygon Optimization}

All curved-path optimization is carried out with \texttt{dnn-mode-connectivity} \citep{garipov_dnn_mode_connectivity}.

\paragraph{Bézier runs.}
For the Bézier runs discussed in the main text, the path family is quadratic Bézier with \(\texttt{num\_bends}=3\), so the two endpoints are fixed and one trainable interior point is optimized. The \texttt{seed\{n\}--seed\{m\}}, \texttt{seed\{n\}--mirror}, and \texttt{seed\{n\}--randperm} runs use:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Curve-training epochs:} \(200\)
    \item \textbf{Learning rate:} \(0.015\)
    \item \textbf{Weight decay:} \(5\times 10^{-4}\)
    \item \textbf{Evaluation split:} CIFAR-10 test split (\texttt{use\_test=true})
    \item \textbf{Seed configurations:} seeds \(0,1,2\), giving the three pairwise independently trained runs, together with one mirror-permuted run and one random-permutation run for each seed
\end{itemize}


\paragraph{Codimension-constrained polygonal-chain runs.}
For the polygonal-chain experiments, the same two-endpoint \texttt{seed0--seed1} setup is used, but the path family is \texttt{PolyChain} with \(\texttt{num\_bends}=3\). The main settings are identical to the Bézier runs above.
In the random-hyperplane experiments, the trainable midpoint is additionally projected after each optimizer step to a random affine subspace through the interpolation midpoint, with codimensions \(k \in \{1,5,10,30\}\). All other polygon-training settings are kept fixed across those codimensions.


\section{Reproducibility Details for the Controlled LMC Benchmark}
\label{app:controlled_benchmark_repro}


The controlled LMC benchmark uses the same \texttt{pytorch-vgg-cifar10} setup \citep{fu_pytorch_vgg_cifar10} with the same setting as the experiments above to train the \texttt{VGG16/CIFAR-10} endpoint models. The core settings for the rest of the experiment are:


After the split, the two branches differ not only in minibatch order but also in the sequence of random data augmentations. The shared-training splits used are \(100/100\), \(80/120\), \(30/170\), \(8/192\), \(4/196\), and \(0/200\). In the \(0/200\) case, the shared checkpoint is just the random initialization saved at epoch \(0\) together with the optimizer state so that split training can resume from it. In the other cases, a shared checkpoint is first trained for \(4\), \(8\), \(30\), \(80\), or \(100\) epochs with shared seed \(42\), and then split training continues to epoch \(200\) with split seeds \(0\) and \(1\). In the shared-training stage the script explicitly sets \texttt{lr\_schedule\_epochs=200} so that the learning-rate schedule is the same as if the model had been trained continuously for the full \(200\) epochs. The same plot also includes an \texttt{independent} baseline, constructed from separately trained \texttt{seed0} and \texttt{seed1} endpoint checkpoints packaged into the same evaluation format.

The alignment benchmark itself is run on the resulting endpoint pair \((\theta_0,\theta_1)\), with:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Artificial permutation:} a fixed random full-network VGG16 permutation with \texttt{perm\_seed=42}, applied to \(\theta_1\) to construct \(\theta_1'\).
    \item \textbf{Weight matching iterations:} at most \(100\).
    \item \textbf{Weight-matching seeds for variance checks:} \texttt{wm\_seed} \(\in \{0,1,2\}\), which changes the permutation-update order inside weight matching.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reproducibility Details for XOR Experiments}
\label{app:xor_repro}

\subsection{XOR Alignment Experiment}

The core settings are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dataset:} the full four-point XOR set \(\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\}\) with binary labels \(\{0,1,1,0\}\).
    \item \textbf{Architecture:} fully connected ReLU MLP with one hidden layer and one scalar output logit.
    \item \textbf{Parameter initialization:} uniform in \([-0.5,0.5]\).
    \item \textbf{Optimizer:} full-batch SGD.
    \item \textbf{Learning rate:} \(0.05\).
    \item \textbf{Maximum training length:} \(5000\) epochs.
    \item \textbf{Scheduler:} \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} with factor \(0.5\), patience \(200\), threshold \(10^{-4}\), and minimum learning rate \(5\times 10^{-4}\).
    \item \textbf{Stopping rule:} stop once the loss fails to improve by at least \(10^{-4}\) for \(500\) epochs.
\end{itemize}

For the minimal-width run reported in the thesis, the hidden size is \(2\), and up to \(10\) independently initialized seeds are used, giving \(45\) unordered model pairs. For each pair, the exhaustive alignment search checks both valid hidden-unit permutations: the identity and the swap.

\subsection{XOR Mode-Connectivity Experiment}

Endpoint training uses the same XOR dataset and the same SGD-based training procedure described above. The curve-fitting script filters trained endpoints by requiring \(100\%\) accuracy. The default script configuration uses \(10\) candidate seeds and hidden size \(3\). The same code was used with hidden size \(2\) for the minimal-width test and hidden size \(3\) for the next-width comparison.

Two path families are considered: quadratic Bézier curves and polygonal chains. In the default configuration, both use \(\texttt{num\_bends}=3\), which means that the path has a single trainable interior point. The main curve-fitting settings are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Optimization steps:} \(1500\)
    \item \textbf{Learning rate:} \(0.05\)
    \item \textbf{Weight decay:} \(0\)
    \item \textbf{Samples of \(t\) inside the fitting objective:} \(31\)
    \item \textbf{Default objective:} minimize max loss along the sampled path
\end{itemize}
When a single trainable point did not suffice, the number of trainable points was increased by using more bends, up to \(10\) trainable points in the reported search.

\section{Reproducibility Details for Scale-Aware Sinkhorn Experiments}
\label{app:sinkhorn_scale_repro}

\subsection{XOR Permutation and Scale Alignment}

The XOR permutation-and-scale experiments use the same XOR endpoint-training routine described above. The retained endpoint widths are \(h \in \{3,5,7\}\), with \(21\), \(36\), and \(10\) valid pairs respectively. For every pair, linear interpolation is evaluated on \(61\) points, while the optimization objective is formed from \(31\) sampled interpolation points.

The default alignment settings are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sinkhorn permutation only:} Adam, \(500\) steps, learning rate \(0.01\), \(\tau=1.0\), \(20\) Sinkhorn iterations, identity-strength \(1.0\), early-stopping patience \(100\), minimum delta \(10^{-6}\).
    \item \textbf{Best permutation + scale refinement:} Adam, \(500\) steps, learning rate \(0.01\), scale regularization \(10^{-3}\), early-stopping patience \(150\), minimum delta \(10^{-6}\).
    \item \textbf{Sinkhorn permutation + scale:} Adam, \(500\) steps, learning rate \(0.01\), \(\tau=1.0\), \(20\) Sinkhorn iterations, identity-strength \(1.0\), scale regularization \(10^{-3}\), early-stopping patience \(100\), minimum delta \(10^{-6}\).
\end{itemize}

If the default run fails to satisfy the target criterion, a bounded hyperparameter search is triggered. The search grids are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sinkhorn permutation only:} steps \(\{500\}\), learning rate \(\{0.01,0.1\}\), \(\tau \in \{1.0,2.0\}\), identity-strength \(\{1.0\}\), search patience \(50\), search minimum delta \(10^{-3}\). The target is the best exhaustive-permutation loss barrier for the same pair.
    \item \textbf{Best permutation + scale refinement:} steps \(\{500\}\), learning rate \(\{0.01,0.005\}\), scale regularization \(\{0,10^{-3}\}\), search patience \(50\), search minimum delta \(10^{-3}\), target \(\epsilon=0.01\).
    \item \textbf{Sinkhorn permutation + scale:} steps \(\{500\}\), learning rate \(\{0.01,0.1\}\), \(\tau \in \{1.0,2.0\}\), identity-strength \(\{1.0\}\), scale regularization \(\{0,5\times10^{-4},10^{-3}\}\), search patience \(50\), search minimum delta \(10^{-3}\), target \(\epsilon=0.01\).
\end{itemize}

Because the best setting is selected separately for each pair, there is no single global optimum shared by all pairs.
