\begin{abstract}
To analyze the loss landscape of neural networks, one has to take the weight symmetries into account. Neurons can be permuted without changing the function represented by the network. Therefore, when assessing whether two networks are similar, one must account for these symmetries. If, even after reparametrization or re-basing, interpolated networks show high loss (i.e. a high barrier), this may indicate that they lie in separate basins of the loss landscape and represent fundamentally different solutions. However, it has been conjectured that, for independently trained neural networks, some permutation re-basing removes such barriers entirely. We test this claim in ReLU networks using exhaustive experiments where permutation-search error can be ruled out. We find in small networks trained on the XOR problem perfectly accurate solutions that are fundamentally in different basins, providing a concrete counterexample to a universal permutation-based account of linear mode connectivity. We then enlarge the symmetry class with function-preserving positive scaling. We introduce both a joint Sinkhorn permutation-and-scaling method and a method-agnostic refinement that holds a chosen permutation fixed and optimizes only its weight scales. In these small networks, for which all permutations can be enumerated, scaling improves even the best permutation; across VGG/CIFAR-10 and MLP/Fashion-MNIST, it consistently reduces both weight-matching and Sinkhorn training-loss barriers. Nonlinear path experiments further separate weight symmetry from architectural redundancy: the same small XOR networks stay separated even when nonlinear Bézier and polygonal loss paths are used, whereas adding hidden units creates low-loss paths and makes weight scaling useful for path optimization. These results show that permutation symmetry is an incomplete explanation of the connectivity of neural-network loss basins and that both the optimization of functionally equivalent model representatives and the available model capacity determine whether low-loss paths are found.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Are independently trained neural networks genuinely in different loss basins, or
do they only appear separated because their hidden features use different
coordinates?  The question matters both for the geometry of neural-network
loss landscapes and for operations such as weight averaging.  A high-loss
straight line between two parameter vectors is not conclusive: neural-network
parameterizations are non-identifiable, and functionally equivalent
representatives can have very different interpolation behavior.

The single-basin-modulo-permutation conjecture proposes that most stochastic
gradient descent (SGD) solutions can be permuted into linear mode connectivity
(LMC)~\citep{entezari2021role}.  Re-basing methods make this hypothesis
operational and substantially reduce barriers in MLPs and convolutional
networks~\citep{ainsworth2023git,guerrero2023rebasin}.  Yet a residual barrier
after alignment is ambiguous.  An algorithm may have missed a suitable
permutation, the permutation symmetry class may itself be too small, or the
architecture may lack the representational freedom needed for a low-loss path.
Large-network studies cannot cleanly distinguish these explanations because
their permutation groups are combinatorial and alignment is approximate.

\newpage
We isolate these mechanisms using small networks trained on the XOR problem,
whose size permits exhaustive permutation search, then test the resulting
account in VGG networks.  The
minimal-width experiment first removes the search confound: some trained pairs
remain classification-incompatible under every permutation.  To check that
this behavior is not specific to deterministic full-batch optimization, we
observe it with both full-batch gradient descent (GD) and minibatch SGD.  We then
enlarge the exact symmetry class using positive ReLU scaling.  Starting from
the exhaustive best permutation and optimizing scales alone identifies gains
that cannot be attributed to better correspondence search.  Finally, nonlinear
path and width experiments separate representative choice from architectural
redundancy: symmetry moves within an equivalence class, while extra units add
routes through parameter space.
Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We give an exhaustive counterexample to universal permutation-based
    LMC for trained networks.  At minimal XOR width, every pair of trained
    solutions not already nearly identical up to a hidden-unit permutation
    fails classification-LMC under exhaustive alignment.
    \item We introduce scale-aware alignment and show, after exhaustive
    permutation search, that positive scaling lowers the loss barrier for all
    tested pairs of networks trained on XOR at widths $3$, $5$, and $7$.
    \item We show an empirical transition in nonlinear connectivity between
    widths two and three, distinguishing representational redundancy from
    symmetry alignment.  Scaling both endpoints does not overcome the
    width-$2$ failure, but lowers the mean barrier at every tested width above
    two for both polygonal and B\'ezier parameterizations.
    \item We show that scaling improves over permutation-only alignment on
    three independently trained pairs for each of VGG11/13/16/19 on
    CIFAR-10 and a ten-layer MLP on Fashion-MNIST.
\end{itemize}

\section{Problem Formulation and Related Work}
\label{sec:problem}

\paragraph{Connectivity and interpolation barriers.}
For endpoints $\theta_A,\theta_B\in\Theta$, linear interpolation is
$\theta(\lambda)=(1-\lambda)\theta_A+\lambda\theta_B$, with
$\lambda\in[0,1]$.  Following \citet{entezari2021role}, the barrier is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
B(\theta_A,\theta_B)
={}&\sup_{\lambda\in[0,1]}
\Big[C\big((1-\lambda)\theta_A+\lambda\theta_B\big)\\
&-\big[(1-\lambda)C(\theta_A)+\lambda C(\theta_B)\big]\Big].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:barrier}
\end{equation}
Here $C:\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ is a scalar cost assigned to a model.  For
gradient-based alignment, $C$ must be differentiable and denotes binary or
multiclass cross-entropy in our experiments.  We use $C$ to follow the
re-basing literature and to distinguish the generic cost from a particular
choice of loss.  Classification error is used only as a reporting cost and is
never optimized.  The supremum is approximated on a finite grid.  Nonlinear
mode connectivity instead asks whether a general curve
$\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow\Theta$ joins the endpoints at low loss.  A curved path
may avoid a region crossed by the straight segment, so nonlinear connectivity
does not imply LMC~\citep{draxler2018essentially,garipov2018loss}.

\paragraph{Permutation re-basing.}
Relabeling a hidden layer and applying the inverse relabeling to the next
layer preserves the network function; the same action reorders channels in
convolutional networks.  Weight matching finds layerwise permutations that
reduce Euclidean distance, while activation matching aligns representations on
data~\citep{ainsworth2023git}.  Sinkhorn re-basing instead relaxes permutation
matrices to differentiable, approximately doubly stochastic assignments that
can be optimized against an interpolation objective
\citep{guerrero2023rebasin}.  These successes support a symmetry-aware view of
loss landscapes, but a residual barrier obtained by a heuristic cannot
establish that the symmetry class itself is insufficient.

\paragraph{Why linear connectivity matters.}
A curved connection establishes geometric connectedness, but a straight path
is the relevant object for parameter averaging.  Checkpoint averaging can
improve generalization within one trajectory~\citep{izmailov2018averaging}, and
averaging related fine-tuned models can be effective without ensemble-time
cost~\citep{wortsman2022model}.  Our focus is the geometric prerequisite of a
low-barrier segment, rather than a claim that an aligned midpoint must
outperform its endpoints.

\section{Scale-Aware Alignment}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Permutation action.}
For a network with $L$ affine layers, let
$h_\ell=\sigma(W_\ell h_{\ell-1}+b_\ell)$ for hidden layers, where $\sigma$
acts coordinatewise, and let $P_\ell$ relabel hidden layer $\ell$.  The induced
action on adjacent weights and biases is
\begin{equation}
\widetilde W_\ell=P_\ell W_\ell P_{\ell-1}^{\top},\qquad
\widetilde b_\ell=P_\ell b_\ell.
\label{eq:hard-permutation-action}
\end{equation}
Because $P_\ell^{-1}=P_\ell^\top$ and coordinatewise activations satisfy
$\sigma(P_\ell z)=P_\ell\sigma(z)$,
Eq.~\ref{eq:hard-permutation-action} preserves the network function.

\paragraph{Original Sinkhorn re-basing.}

Sinkhorn re-basing replaces each discrete $P_\ell$ with a differentiable soft
assignment~\citep{guerrero2023rebasin}.  An unconstrained score matrix $Z_\ell$
is mapped to an approximately doubly stochastic matrix
$S_\ell=\operatorname{Sinkhorn}(Z_\ell/\tau)$, where the temperature $\tau$
controls its sharpness.  It optimizes the layerwise scores using the relaxed
action
\begin{equation}
\widetilde W_\ell^{\mathrm{soft}}
=S_\ell W_\ell S_{\ell-1}^{\top},\qquad
\widetilde b_\ell^{\mathrm{soft}}=S_\ell b_\ell.
\label{eq:soft-permutation-action}
\end{equation}
Gradients through the Sinkhorn map are obtained by implicit
differentiation.  Since $S_\ell^\top$ is generally not $S_\ell^{-1}$, the soft
action need not preserve the endpoint function.  Linear assignment projects
each $S_\ell$ to a hard, function-preserving permutation for evaluation.

\paragraph{Scale-aware extension.}
Permutation is only one class of exact symmetry in neural networks.  Another
is the scaling symmetry induced by positive homogeneity: for ReLU,
$\sigma(cz)=c\sigma(z)$ for $c>0$, so the incoming weights and bias of a unit
can be multiplied by $c$, provided that its outgoing weights are divided by
$c$.  Applying permutation and positive scaling together is therefore an exact
symmetry, selecting a different parameter-space representative of the same
endpoint function.  REPAIR also uses channel-wise scaling and shifting, but
applies it to interpolated activations and thereby changes the represented
function~\citep{jordan2023repair}.  Consequently, the intermediate networks
obtained by linearly interpolating to that endpoint can have different losses,
and hence a different barrier.  This motivates studying connectivity under a
richer symmetry class before attributing a residual barrier to genuinely
distinct basins.

We extend Sinkhorn because it already expresses permutation search as a
continuous, differentiable optimization, so continuous scale variables can be
added and trained jointly under the same interpolation objective.  This choice
does not imply that other re-basing methods cannot incorporate scaling.
Doing so in layerwise matching or straight-through methods would instead
require modifying their matching objectives or coupling discrete permutation
updates with continuous scale optimization, making the extension less direct.

For each hidden layer, we introduce log-scales $u_\ell$ and set
$D_\ell=\operatorname{diag}(e^{u_\ell})$.  The exponential
enforces the positivity required by ReLU homogeneity.  Combining $D_\ell$ with
a hard permutation gives the following function-preserving transformation:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde W_\ell
=P_\ell D_\ell W_\ell D_{\ell-1}^{-1}P_{\ell-1}^{\top},
\qquad
\widetilde b_\ell=P_\ell D_\ell b_\ell.
\label{eq:hard-monomial-action}
\end{equation}
Positive homogeneity propagates the scaled, permuted activation to the next
layer, where the inverse factor cancels it; hence the transformed endpoint
computes the same function.

During joint soft optimization, our implementation uses the corresponding
relaxation
\begin{equation}
\widehat W_\ell
=S_\ell D_\ell W_\ell D_{\ell-1}^{-1}S_{\ell-1}^{\top},
\qquad
\widehat b_\ell=S_\ell D_\ell b_\ell.
\label{eq:soft-monomial-action}
\end{equation}
Only the diagonal scaling is inverted exactly; $S_{\ell-1}^{\top}$ remains the
soft surrogate used by the original method.  Projecting $S_\ell$ to $P_\ell$
recovers the exact action in Eq.~\ref{eq:hard-monomial-action}.

We consider two variants.  \emph{Permutation + scale (joint Sinkhorn)}
optimizes $Z_\ell$ and $u_\ell$ together.  \emph{Permutation + scale
(fixed-permutation refinement)} first obtains and freezes a hard permutation,
then optimizes $u_\ell$ alone.  A quadratic penalty on $u_\ell$ controls the unbounded
scaling symmetry and discourages numerically ill-conditioned representatives.
Extending the standard re-basin notation $\pi_P(\theta_B)$, let
$\pi_{Z,u}(\theta_B)$ denote the endpoint transformed by the current soft
assignments and scales.  Following the original Sinkhorn
method~\citep{guerrero2023rebasin}, we use the interpolation cost
\begin{equation}
 C_\lambda(Z,u;\theta_A,\theta_B)
 =C\!\left((1-\lambda)\theta_A
 +\lambda\pi_{Z,u}(\theta_B)\right).
\label{eq:interpolation-cost}
\end{equation}
Fixing $\lambda=0.5$ gives the midpoint objective; drawing
$\lambda\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)$ independently at each update gives the random-point
objective.  The midpoint is inexpensive and often the most vulnerable
location, whereas random sampling covers the path across updates.  We minimize
Eq.~\ref{eq:interpolation-cost} plus
$\lambda_s\sum_\ell\lVert u_\ell\rVert_2^2$, where the sum is over hidden
layers.  Freezing a hard permutation removes $Z$ from the optimization.  We
report barriers only after projecting the soft assignments to hard
permutations.  Experiment-specific objectives and grids are given in
Appendix~\ref{app:details}.

\section{Controlled XOR Studies}
\label{sec:xor}

All studies in this section use one-hidden-layer ReLU networks with biases and
one output logit, trained on the four XOR examples.  Endpoints are initialized
independently and retained only if they reach $100\%$ classification accuracy
and low binary cross-entropy.  Unless stated otherwise, endpoint training uses
full-batch GD, and connectivity is evaluated using the barrier in
Eq.~\ref{eq:barrier}.  This controlled setting lets us enumerate the complete
hidden-unit permutation group at every studied width.  Full training and
optimization details appear in Appendix~\ref{app:details}.

\subsection{Permutation Symmetry Is Insufficient}

When permutation-based LMC fails, did the alignment algorithm fail, or does no
permutation work?  We first answer this in the smallest nontrivial setting,
width $h=2$.  There are only $2!=2$ hidden-unit permutations, so the symmetry
class can be searched exactly.

\paragraph{Training and evaluation.}
As a robustness check against the interpretation that the effect is specific
to deterministic optimization, we repeat the default full-batch protocol with
minibatch SGD using minibatches containing two of the four XOR examples and
reshuffling each epoch.  For each
protocol, eight successful endpoints give $28$ pairs.  We enumerate identity
and swap and evaluate classification error along each interpolation.

\paragraph{Result.}
For both training protocols, only $6/28$ pairs preserve $100\%$ accuracy along
the best-permuted segment; the remaining $22/28$ incur interpolation error
under both possible permutations.  Figure~\ref{fig:xor-counterexample} shows a
successful pair and a representative full-batch failure.  In the failure,
both endpoints correctly implement XOR and have similar decision boundaries,
but no hidden-unit relabeling makes their straight interpolation compatible.
Because the search is exhaustive, this result cannot be attributed to weight
matching, Sinkhorn relaxation, or alignment initialization.

These trained endpoints refute a universal claim that permutation-based LMC
follows automatically from SGD or GD.  They do not contradict the
sufficiently-wide, high-probability form of the single-basin-modulo-permutation
conjecture.  They also qualify the
interpretation of the hand-constructed counterexample of
\citet{ainsworth2023git}.  Ainsworth et al.\ used adversarial, non-SGD
endpoints to show that architecture alone does not guarantee
permutation-based LMC, and argued that the empirical prevalence of LMC may
therefore reflect an implicit bias of SGD.  Our exhaustive result shows that
this bias is not absolute: standard minibatch SGD and full-batch GD can
themselves produce successful solutions for which no permutation yields LMC.

\subsection{Positive Scaling Beyond Permutation}
\label{sec:scale-results}

Does positive scaling improve compatibility once permutation-search error has
been removed?  Using the shared full-batch protocol at widths $h=3,5,7$ gives
$21$, $36$, and $10$ valid endpoint pairs.  For each pair we enumerate all
$h!$ permutations, select the one with the smallest sampled loss barrier, and
then freeze that permutation while optimizing only positive scales.  Final
barriers use $61$ interpolation points.  We also report raw interpolation and
practical Sinkhorn baselines.

\paragraph{Width and permutation alignment.}

Increasing width changes the result.  In the initial exhaustive sweep over
$h\in\{2,3,4,5\}$, the number of pairs maintaining $100\%$ accuracy along the
best-permuted interpolation rises steadily; at $h=5$, every evaluated pair
maintains perfect accuracy.  Perfect accuracy does not mean zero loss barrier:
midpoint confidence can still fall relative to the endpoints.

This width trend is consistent with Git Re-Basin, which empirically found lower
post-alignment barriers for wider VGG and ResNet models
\citep{ainsworth2023git}.  Our purpose here is not to re-establish that trend,
but to measure it under exhaustive permutation search and then isolate the
additional effect of scaling.  Table~\ref{tab:xor-scale} reports all aggregate
results.  The exhaustive-permutation mean falls from $0.357$ at $h=3$ to
$0.023$ at $h=7$; practical Sinkhorn remains above the exhaustive result at
each width, so failure of the heuristic alone cannot establish that no suitable
permutation exists.

\paragraph{Variability across pairs.}
Barriers are nonnegative and strongly right-skewed: some independently trained
pairs are almost connected, whereas others remain more disconnected.  For
example, at $h=5$ the joint
Sinkhorn+scale barrier has median $0.011$ but ranges up to $0.692$, yielding
$0.074\pm0.149$.  Pairs share endpoints, so these statistics are descriptive.

\paragraph{Scale beyond exhaustive permutation.}
The decisive comparison is between exhaustive permutation and the same frozen
permutation followed by scale refinement.  Refinement lowers the mean barrier
by $31$--$78\%$ across widths and lowers it on all $67$ evaluated pairs.  Since
all permutations have already been enumerated and the selected permutation is
held fixed during refinement, the comparison isolates the additional rescaling
freedom.  For these endpoints, rescaling reaches functionally equivalent
parameterizations with lower interpolation barriers than are attainable by
permutation alone.

Joint optimization is more mixed at the smallest width.  It achieves a lower
mean barrier than exhaustive permutation at $h=5$ and $h=7$, but not at $h=3$.
Its gap to fixed-permutation scale refinement is therefore an optimization gap:
the richer symmetry class contains better representatives even when a
from-scratch solver does not reliably find them.
Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:xor-profiles} shows the corresponding mean
interpolation profiles.  The curves visualize the width trend and method
ranking summarized more completely by Table~\ref{tab:xor-scale}.

\subsection{Width Enables Nonlinear Connectivity}
\label{sec:nonlinear}

Residual linear barriers need not imply disconnected low-loss sets, so we test
both the polygonal and quadratic B\'ezier paths used by
\citet{garipov2018loss}.  For endpoints $\theta_A,\theta_B$ and one trainable
interior control point $v$, their one-control-point parameterizations are
\begin{equation}
\phi^{\mathrm{poly}}_{v}(\lambda)=
\begin{cases}
2\bigl(\lambda v+(\tfrac12-\lambda)\theta_A\bigr),
    & 0\leq\lambda\leq\tfrac12,\\
2\bigl((\lambda-\tfrac12)\theta_B+(1-\lambda)v\bigr),
    & \tfrac12\leq\lambda\leq1,
\end{cases}
\label{eq:polygonal-path}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\phi^{\mathrm{B\acute ezier}}_{v}(\lambda)
=(1-\lambda)^2\theta_A+2\lambda(1-\lambda)v+\lambda^2\theta_B.
\label{eq:bezier-path}
\end{equation}
The polygonal path consists of two line segments meeting at $v$; the B\'ezier
path bends smoothly under its influence.  We keep the endpoints fixed and
optimize the control points to minimize the maximum loss over $31$ sampled
path locations, using up to ten interior points.  To test whether width affects
available routes rather than only alignment, we apply these paths to endpoints
from the shared protocol.  At widths $h\in\{2,3,5,7\}$, we also
compare a one-control-point path with joint optimization of that path and
independent positive scales at both endpoints.  Scaling preserves the endpoint
functions; no permutation alignment is applied in this comparison.

\paragraph{Result.}
At $h=2$, extensive searches find low-loss nonlinear paths only between
essentially identical endpoints; additional interior points do not recover
nontrivial connections.  At $h=3$, all studied endpoint pairs are connected by
low-loss paths that retain $100\%$ accuracy throughout interpolation.
Figure~\ref{fig:nonlinear-width} contrasts representative outcomes.  This
result is empirical, not a proof that no width-$2$ path exists
outside the tested families.  The only two successful width-$2$ endpoint pairs
correspond to seed pairs $(2,9)$ and $(5,12)$.  These pairs are nearly duplicate
parameter solutions: their Euclidean endpoint
separations are only about $0.6\%$ of the corresponding parameter norms, and
both path families attain barriers at numerical zero, providing no evidence of
a nontrivial connection between distinct width-$2$ solutions.  A plausible
explanation for the remaining failures is that width $2$ is
capacity-tight for XOR.  The nontrivial endpoint pairs have different decision-
boundary geometries, each constructed from only two ReLU boundary pieces.

\noindent Within the tested path families, continuously transforming one
geometry into the other with no spare hidden unit requires an intermediate
arrangement that misclassifies at least one XOR point.
After adding a third unit, all studied pairs admit low-loss paths, consistent
with the extra unit providing the
temporary representational freedom needed during this transformation.  This
supports a distinction that endpoint alignment alone cannot make: symmetry
selects among equivalent representatives at fixed capacity, whereas width
changes the representational resources available along a path.

\vspace{-1.5ex}
\paragraph{Endpoint-scaling result.}
Endpoint scaling does not remove the minimal-width obstruction.  With one
interior control point, both polygonal and B\'ezier searches find low-loss
connections for only $2/28$ width-$2$ pairs, with or without jointly optimized
endpoint scales.

At larger widths, scaling nevertheless improves path finding.  For polygonal
paths, it lowers the mean barrier by $1.3\%$, $4.7\%$, and $94.9\%$ at widths
$3$, $5$, and $7$, respectively.  Quadratic B\'ezier paths show the same
qualitative higher-width pattern: little change at width $3$, a modest
reduction at width $5$, and a reduction of more than $90\%$ at width $7$.
The two path families also give identical low-loss connection counts at every
width.  At width $2$, their mean-barrier details differ slightly---scaling
improves the polygonal mean but worsens the B\'ezier mean---yet neither finds
additional low-loss connections.  Figure~\ref{fig:nonlinear-scale-width}
shows the polygonal comparison; the visually near-identical B\'ezier profile,
which supports the same conclusions, is omitted.

\section{Larger-Network Validation}
\label{sec:vgg}

\paragraph{Setup.}
We evaluate VGG11, VGG13, VGG16, and VGG19 on CIFAR-10, and a ten-layer
width-512 MLP on Fashion-MNIST.  For every architecture--dataset combination,
six independently trained models form three disjoint endpoint pairs: runs
initialized with seeds $(0,1)$, $(2,3)$, and $(4,5)$.
As a data-independent permutation baseline, weight matching (WM) approximately
solves
\begin{equation}
P^\star\in\arg\min_{P_1,\ldots,P_{L-1}}
\left\lVert\theta_A-\pi_P(\theta_B)\right\rVert_2^2,
\label{eq:weight-matching}
\end{equation}
where $\pi_P$ applies the hidden-layer relabelings of
Eq.~\ref{eq:hard-permutation-action}.  The standard solver
cycles through layers, solving one linear assignment problem while holding the
remaining permutations fixed~\citep{ainsworth2023git}.  For WM with scale
refinement, we fix the resulting permutation and optimize only the
function-preserving positive scales.

We compare raw
interpolation, weight matching, weight matching followed by positive-scale
refinement, Sinkhorn alignment, joint Sinkhorn--scale optimization, and
positive-scale refinement of the projected Sinkhorn permutation.  Each
optimized method receives a pair-specific 12-candidate search: fitting and
early stopping use one validation half, selection uses the other, and the
selected configuration is refit at full budget.  Alignment fitting uses the
complete endpoint-training split (45,000 examples for CIFAR-10 and 55,000 for
Fashion-MNIST).  Figure~\ref{fig:vgg} evaluates the resulting paths on a fixed,
class-balanced 10,000-example subset of that split.  The test set is first
accessed after all choices are frozen and is reported separately in
Appendix~\ref{app:larger-network}.
Because the datasets and architectures differ, comparisons are made between
methods within each model rather than between absolute barrier magnitudes
across groups.

\paragraph{Result.}
Figure~\ref{fig:vgg} reports the mean training-loss barrier across the three
pairs.  Positive-scale refinement reduces the WM barrier from
$0.531\mathbin{\pm}0.057$, $0.975\mathbin{\pm}0.045$,
$1.157\mathbin{\pm}0.138$, and $1.677\mathbin{\pm}0.184$ to
$0.187\mathbin{\pm}0.006$, $0.156\mathbin{\pm}0.012$,
$0.206\mathbin{\pm}0.018$, and $0.277\mathbin{\pm}0.030$ on
VGG11/13/16/19, respectively.  Refining a fixed Sinkhorn permutation also
consistently outperforms joint optimization: its VGG11/13/16/19 means are
$0.625$, $0.787$, $0.912$, and $0.957$, compared with $0.760$, $0.911$,
$1.089$, and $1.089$ for the joint variant.  On Fashion-MNIST, scale refinement
reduces WM from $1.971\mathbin{\pm}0.810$ to
$0.259\mathbin{\pm}0.052$ and Sinkhorn from
$0.584\mathbin{\pm}0.068$ to $0.382\mathbin{\pm}0.188$.  Thus scaling selects
substantially better representatives, but none of the tested methods eliminates
the sampled training-loss barrier.

These results transfer the controlled XOR finding, although permutation search
is non-exhaustive, three pairs only coarsely estimate variability, and
hyperparameters are selected per method and pair.  Still, the cross-model
pattern supports scaling beyond XOR.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

\paragraph{Symmetry and redundancy play different roles.}
Permutation removes discrete coordinate mismatch.  Positive scaling differs:
it is connected to the identity by a zero-loss exact-symmetry orbit and
therefore cannot change whether an unrestricted continuous low-loss connection
exists.  In the nonlinear experiments, it can only make such a connection
easier to express or find within the tested path families and optimization
procedures.  Width, by contrast, changes the architecture's representational
freedom and can create routes unavailable to a narrower network; curved paths
relax the separate requirement that such a route be straight.

The minimal-width experiment also sharpens the interpretation of
\citet{ainsworth2023git}.  Their hand-constructed counterexample using the XOR
problem shows that architecture alone does not guarantee permutation-based LMC, and they suggest
that LMC may be an artifact of SGD.  Our result rules out a broad reading under
which standard gradient-based training itself guarantees alignable endpoints:
neither minibatch SGD nor full-batch GD prevents permutation-incompatible
successful solutions.  It does not rule out an SGD bias toward compatibility
in wider or typical modern networks.  Indeed, the rapid fall in the exhaustive
permutation barrier with increasing width is consistent with the sufficiently-wide, high-probability
form of the single-basin-modulo-permutation
conjecture~\citep{entezari2021role,ainsworth2023git}.

\paragraph{Optimization versus existence.}
The exhaustive protocol is central to this interpretation.  Without it, a high
barrier after Sinkhorn or weight matching could always be ascribed to search
failure.  Conversely, the gap between joint Sinkhorn and fixed-permutation
scale refinement warns against treating a failed continuous relaxation as
evidence that a good functionally equivalent representative does not exist.
Future work should distinguish the
expressivity of a transformation class from the solver used to search it.

\paragraph{Implications for alignment studies.}
A residual barrier should be interpreted in stages.  Search error must first
be separated from limitations of the transformation class.  If permutation is
insufficient, richer exact symmetries should be tested before inferring distinct
basins.  Linear and nonlinear paths should then be distinguished, and width
should be treated as a change in representational resources rather than merely
a larger matching problem.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The exact conclusions come from small one-hidden-layer networks trained on the
XOR problem.  The
minibatch and full-batch protocols are robustness replications, not a controlled
estimate of stochasticity: with the same number of epochs, minibatches containing
two of the four XOR examples yield two optimizer updates per epoch, whereas
full-batch GD yields one.  The larger-network study is non-exhaustive, uses
only three disjoint pairs per architecture, and relies on pair- and
method-specific hyperparameter selection.  Our nonlinear path searches can
certify found paths but cannot certify nonexistence.  The scale-aware soft
transformation is exactly function preserving only after projection; during
Sinkhorn optimization it is a relaxation.  Finally, we consider ReLU networks,
for which positive homogeneity holds, and do not address normalization layers,
residual architectures, attention symmetries, or approximate functional
equivalences.  Larger multi-seed studies and comparisons with activation repair
are needed before drawing broad architectural conclusions.

\section{Conclusion}

A central problem in studying neural-network loss landscapes is determining
whether barriers between independently trained solutions reflect genuine basin
separation or removable parameterization mismatch.
Permutation symmetry is an important but incomplete explanation of neural-
network connectivity.  Exhaustive XOR search shows that trained endpoints can
remain incompatible under every permutation, for both minibatch SGD and
full-batch GD.  Positive scaling improves even the exhaustive best permutation,
and it also lowers optimized nonlinear-path barriers at larger widths.
However, scaling does not recover low-loss polygonal or B\'ezier connections
for the difficult width-$2$ pairs.  The width-dependent nonlinear experiment
therefore shows that exact symmetries do not subsume representational
constraints.  The same training-loss benefit of scale-aware linear alignment transfers to fixed
VGG/CIFAR-10 and MLP/Fashion-MNIST pairs.  The central distinction is therefore
simple: symmetry determines which representatives we compare; redundancy
determines which paths are available.

\section{Reproducibility Details}
\label{app:details}

\subsection{XOR training and exhaustive alignment}

The XOR set is $\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\}$ with labels
$\{0,1,1,0\}$.  Models have one ReLU hidden layer, biases, and one output logit.
The primary endpoint protocol uses full-batch GD (implemented with the SGD
optimizer) at learning rate $0.05$ for at most $5000$ epochs.
\texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} uses factor $0.5$, patience $200$, threshold
$10^{-4}$, and minimum learning rate $5\times10^{-4}$.  Training stops when
loss has not improved by $10^{-4}$ for $500$ epochs.  For each retained pair,
all $h!$ permutations are evaluated.  Interpolation selection prioritizes
classification error in the width-$2$ diagnostic and loss barrier in the
scale-aware study.

The width-$2$ robustness replication uses true minibatch SGD with minibatches
containing two of the four XOR examples and reshuffles them every epoch.  It otherwise uses the
same initialization range, learning-rate schedule, stopping rule, maximum of
$5000$ epochs, random seeds, and exhaustive identity/swap evaluation.  Eight
successful endpoints produce $28$ pairs in each protocol.  Matching epoch
counts does not match optimizer-update counts: the minibatch protocol makes two
updates per epoch and is therefore a robustness comparison, not an isolated
causal test of stochastic gradient noise.  The width-$3/5/7$ scale-aware study
uses the primary full-batch-GD endpoints.

For nonlinear paths, both quadratic B\'ezier curves and polygonal chains are
optimized for $1500$ steps at learning rate $0.05$, with no weight decay and
$31$ path samples.  The default path has one trainable interior point; harder
pairs use up to ten.  The endpoint-scaling comparison uses one interior point,
raw endpoints without permutation, and independently optimized positive scales
at both endpoints.  Path parameters and endpoint log-scales are optimized
jointly with Adam for $1500$ steps at learning rate $0.05$; the scale penalty
is $10^{-4}$, and final barriers are evaluated on $501$ path locations.

\subsection{XOR scale-aware optimization}

At each optimization step, XOR alignment evaluates binary cross-entropy at
$31$ evenly spaced interpolation points.  The implementation subtracts the
mean endpoint loss from each value and backpropagates the largest resulting
excess; thus, the path-sample contribution to the gradient comes from the
currently worst grid point rather than an average over all $31$ locations.
Final reported curves are evaluated on a separate $61$-point grid.  The default
permutation only (Sinkhorn) run uses Adam for $500$ steps,
learning rate $0.01$, temperature $1$, $20$ Sinkhorn iterations, and identity
strength $1$.  Scale refinement uses Adam for $500$ steps, learning rate
$0.01$, and log-scale regularization $10^{-3}$.  Joint optimization uses the
same default budget.  If a default run misses its target, a bounded search over
learning rates, temperatures, and regularization is used; settings are selected
per pair.  The scale target is barrier $\epsilon=0.01$, while permutation-only
Sinkhorn targets that pair's exhaustive barrier.

\subsection{Larger-network experiments}
\label{app:larger-network}

For every architecture, Figure~\ref{fig:vgg} uses the final--final cell for
three disjoint run pairs, $(0,1)$, $(2,3)$, and $(4,5)$, where each integer is
the random seed of a complete independent training run.  Weight matching is
computed for each pair, and every data-dependent alignment or scale-refinement
method is fitted separately.  Every
optimized method uses a 12-candidate grid over its relevant learning rate,
temperature, and scale-regularization parameters.  A frozen validation split
is divided into disjoint tuning and selection halves; the official test set is
not loaded until the winning configuration for every pair has been frozen.
The permutation-then-scale methods optimize only log-scales after fixing the
corresponding hard permutation.  All six methods are evaluated on the same
$61$-point interpolation grid.  Figure~\ref{fig:vgg} reports the mean and
sample standard deviation across the three independent pairs on the fixed
training-report subset; Figure~\ref{fig:vgg-test} reports the corresponding
official-test-set result.

Test-loss barriers are generally smaller than their training-loss counterparts.
This comparison is relative to the endpoint chord, not to zero loss.  In the
Fashion-MNIST pairs, for example, endpoint training losses are
$0.020$--$0.041$, whereas endpoint test losses are $0.681$--$0.809$; an
interior test-loss curve can therefore remain below its much higher endpoint
chord even while the same path rises far above the near-zero training chord.
Consequently, the zero sampled Fashion-MNIST test-loss barriers in
Figure~\ref{fig:vgg-test} do not imply zero training-loss barrier or constant
classification error along the path.
